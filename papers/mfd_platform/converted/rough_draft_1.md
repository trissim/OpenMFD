Microfluidic devices are an invaluable tool for in-vitro cellular neuroscience research, facilitating studies on neuronal development, regeneration, and degeneration. A wide variety of these devices have been designed to perform specific assays that elucidate cellular and molecular mechanisms. Since 2005, the fabrication of these devices using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) soft lithography with SU-8-based molds has been standard. However, most published and commercially available devices remain low-throughput, supporting only a few replicates per device and lacking compatibility with multichannel pipettes, automated liquid handling robots, and high-content screening microscopes. Additionally, the few commercially available high-throughput devices are often prohibitively expensive for most academic research.

A major challenge in fabricating high-throughput microfluidic devices with arrays of large and cleanly formed wells is the manual punching of PDMS, necessary due to the difficulty in creating structures thicker than 1 mm using SU-8. To address this issue, we present a method that combines affordable, commercially available resin 3D printers with SU-8 photolithography using standard clean room equipment. By attaching 3D-printed well features onto a wafer with heat-curing glue, we can integrate large features—ranging from millimeters to centimeters in height—with SU-8 features as fine as 1 μm. This integration allows for the formation of wells during PDMS curing, eliminating the need for manual punching.

Additionally, we provide an open-source Python script designed to create microfluidic device layouts. This software simplifies device design and fabrication by abstracting the complex combinations of low-level operations required to create and modify device layouts. It supports the creation of microchannels, wells, mask alignment features, and 3D-printed features, as well as tiling layouts for high-throughput versions of devices.

Using our microfluidic fabrication platform, we developed a high-throughput device in the format of a 96-well plate to study axonal regeneration. This device enabled selective injury of cortical neuron axons through a chemical axotomy—a reliable new method that can be performed with simple, automatable liquid handling. This platform empowers researchers with access to a clean room and basic Python knowledge to produce their own or published microfluidic device designs in a high-throughput format rapidly and cost-effectively.
