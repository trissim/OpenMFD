Abstract:

Microfluidic devices are an incredibly valuable tool for in-vitro cellular neuroscience research. From neuronal development, to regeneration and degeneration, a wide variety of devices have been designed to perform specific assays to study the cellular and molecular mechanisms of these processes. Fabrication of these devices using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) soft lithography with SU-8 based molds have been reported and are standard since 2005. However, to this day, most published and commercially available devices are low-throughput, only allowing a few replicates per device, and are not compatible with multichannel pipettes, automated liquid handling robots and high-content screening microscopes. Unfortunately, the very few commercially available high-throughput devices are prohibitively expensive for most academic research. The main difficulty in fabricating high-throughput microfluidic devices containing arrays of large and cleanly formed wells is the necessity to manually punch out PDMS to form the wells. This is due to the difficulty in creating structures thicker than 1mm using SU-8. To resolve this issue, we present a method combining the use of affordable commercially available resin 3D printers and SU-8 photolithography using standard clean room equipment. By interfacing 3D printed well features onto the wafer with heat-curing glue, large features up to multiple millimetres to centimeters tall can be interfaced with SU-8 features as fine as 1um. Integration of these large well features on the mold allow for the formation of wells during PDMS curing, entirely skipping the need for punching wells out. Furthermore, we present an open-source python script designed to create microfluidic device layouts. The software will facilitate the device design and fabrication by abstracting the tedious combinations of low-level operations required to create and modify device layouts. This includes the creation of microchannels, wells, mask alignment features, 3D printed features as well as tiling the layout to create high-throughput versions of the device. Using our microfluidic fabrication platform, we made a high-throughput device in the format of a 96-well plate to study axonal regeneration. Using this device, we selectively injured cortical neuron axons through chemical axotomy, a new reliable axotomy method that is performed by simple automatable liquid handling. This platform will enable any researcher with access to a clean room and basic python knowledge to produce their own or published microfluidic devices designs in a high-throughput format in a rapid and cost-effective manner.

Intro:

Microfluidic devices have become essential in cellular neuroscience, bridging the gap between traditional multi-well plate-based in-vitro experiments and in-vivo studies. Unlike conventional methods, microfluidic platforms offer precise spatial control over neuron cultures and their microenvironments, allowing researchers to manipulate and observe cellular behaviors with a level of detail that closely mimics in-vivo conditions. This precision is critical for studying complex neural processes, such as synapse formation, axon guidance, and myelination, in a controlled yet physiologically relevant setting.

Over time, various microfluidic platforms have been developed for specific assays, significantly advancing research in areas like axon regeneration, axon guidance, myelination, and synaptogenesis. One key advancement is the microfluidic platform developed by Taylor et al. for axon regeneration. This device isolates CNS axons in a fluidically controlled environment, allowing for focused studies on axonal injury and regeneration without interference from cell bodies or dendrites. Similarly, another platform by Taylor uses controlled fluid flow to influence axon guidance, showcasing the versatility of microfluidic designs in modeling different neural processes [(Taylor et al., 2005)](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth777).

For myelination studies, the device developed by Ze Zhong Wang separates neuronal somata from axons, enabling selective interaction between axons and oligodendrocytes. This setup mimics in-vivo myelination, providing a controlled environment for studying axon-glia interactions [(Wang et al., 2018)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6200633/). Ainsley Coquinco's work highlights the use of a microfluidic platform for synaptogenesis, which employs a versatile two- or three-compartment design. These designs are adaptable for various assays, enabling precise control over the microenvironment to study synaptic formation and maturation under different conditions ([Coquinco et al., 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S104474311400027X?via%3Dihub)).

Despite the significant advancements in microfluidic device design, particularly for cellular neuroscience applications, fabricating tall cell compartment wells remains a persistent challenge. The traditional use of SU-8 photoresist for mold fabrication, while effective for creating fine microstructures, is inherently limited by its inability to reliably form features thicker than 1 mm without casting. This limitation has necessitated the manual punching of wells from the PDMS layer after casting, a process that is not only labor-intensive but also prone to introducing defects and variability into the final devices. Such manual interventions are particularly problematic in contexts where high-throughput and precision are essential, especially when scaling the device into a multi-well plate format compatible with multi-channel pipettes and high-content screening microscopes. These tools are critical for high-throughput screening, as they allow for simultaneous processing and analysis of multiple samples, thereby increasing research output and efficiency.

To address the challenges associated with SU-8’s thickness limitations, researchers have explored various alternative methods for creating large wells. One approach involves casting ultra-thick SU-8 layers on a glass substrate, followed by alignment with a PDMS mold. While this method can produce thicker features than standard SU-8 lithography, it comes with significant drawbacks. The process is costly, as it requires a new photomask for each mold, and it is technically demanding due to the challenges associated with solvent evaporation, which can induce internal stress and lead to defects in the SU-8 mold. Moreover, the slow rate of solvent evaporation required to minimize internal stress makes the process tediously long, further complicating its practical application ([Takahiro Tamura, 2018](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.7567/1347-4065/ab09d0)).

Another technique explored is the direct 3D printing of tall PDMS walls using a bioprinter ([Janko Kajtez, 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7435242/)). This method allows for the creation of large wells in a straightforward manner, leveraging the precision of bioprinting technology. However, the reliance on bioprinters, which must be used each time a device is fabricated, limits its practicality, especially in settings that require rapid and large-scale production. Additionally, the serial nature of the printing process significantly reduces throughput, making it less ideal for applications requiring high volumes of devices.

Laser cutting has also been employed as a method for creating large wells in PDMS devices ([Andrew W. Holle, 2007](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/4341853)). This technique utilizes CO2 laser cutters to automate the well-forming process, offering precise control and reproducibility. However, the need for low laser power and slow cutting speeds to achieve the necessary resolution (around 200 μm) requires multiple passes, further slowing down the fabrication process. Furthermore, the availability of high-performance laser cutters is limited in many clean rooms, and the appropriate equipment itself is prohibitively expensive for many research laboratories.

Some researchers have explored combining hot embossing and CNC milling with PMMA plastic to interface large and small features on the same mold. This method allows for the integration of complex structures with high precision, but it requires access to both a hot embosser and a CNC mill—equipment that is not readily available in all clean rooms. Additionally, this approach necessitates the creation of an extra master mold, which adds another layer of complexity and cost to the fabrication process ([Park et al., 2011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2864814/)).

Given these limitations, the exploration of 3D printing resin directly onto SU-8 features has gained attention as a potential solution ([Jesper Y. Pan, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590007222000211)). This technique involves printing resin parts onto SU-8 features on a silicon substrate, allowing for the integration of large features with the fine structures of SU-8. However, challenges remain, including the potential for defects at the interface, such as the “elephant foot” phenomenon, where the first layers of resin may spread, leading to inaccuracies. Additionally, the need for large build plates to accommodate 6” wafers and the precise alignment of SU-8 features with the wafer add complexity to this method. This can be done by dicing the wafer following alignment marks around the device features.

Finally, steel 3D printing has been explored for fabricating well features that can be glued onto the mold ([Mervi Ristola, 2019](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1361-6439/ab16a7)). This approach utilizes a lock-and-key mechanism to align the well inserts with SU-8 features, allowing for the seamless creation of large wells during PDMS casting. While this method effectively integrates large features with fine microstructures, it is time-consuming due to the manual placement of individual well inserts, and it requires access to expensive steel 3D printers, which are not commonly available in many clean rooms.

While some high-throughput axon isolation devices are commercially available, their fabrication methods are either not described or mostly suited for industrial fabrication. A patented hard plastic-based microtiter microfluidic plate (MC-plate) containing 96 units of 3-compartment devices in the format of a 384 multi-well plate has been reported ([Moll et al. 2023](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acschemneuro.3c00409)). However, the fabrication requires thermal fusion bonding 3 different layers: a cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) well layer where wells are obtained through CNC milling, a clear microfluidic layer where microchannels are obtained through hot-embossing, and a clear bottom film to seal the bottom of the plate. As previously mentioned, CNC milling and hot embossing require expensive equipment that are not available in most cleanrooms and require a high level of expertise to perform. These fabrications make sense in the context of commercial high-volume fabrication, but not in the context of low-volume fabrication for academic use. The OrganoPlate 3-lane used as a directional 3D neurite outgrowth model for studying motor axon biology and disease, but its fabrication method is not described ([Spijkers et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-81335-z)). Lastly, Enuvio offers the PDMS based OMEGA96 Neuronal Co-Culture Microplate device, but its fabrication method is proprietary and its pricing is prohibitive for most academic use.

In order to enable researchers to reliably and rapidly create high-throughput microfluidic devices for in-vitro neuroscience, we present a fabrication method that allows for creation of a microfluidic mold that contains large well features, enabling rapid and reliable fabrication of devices using the mold with PDMS soft lithography. In this method, we combine elements from Jesper et al. and Mervi et al.’s work to integrate macros features made of heat-resistant 3D printing resin with fine SU-8 features. To avoid the need for manual placement of the inserts onto the wafer, 3D printed well inserts containing a protruding pin acting as a key are printed in the correct position to be aligned with the wafer’s well features containing a hole acting as a lock. Using a detachable magnetic build plate, the inserts are then transferred from the resin 3D printer to a larger build plate to be used for adhesive bonding between the inserts and the wafer containing thin well and microchannels features made of SU-8. Low viscocity heat-curing epoxy is applied in excess to the underside cavity on the pin side of the inserts. The wafer is then placed with the SU-8 features facing the insert’s pin side and aligned until all pins lock into place, leading to successful alignment. Another large build plate is placed above the wafer and c-clamps are used to apply uniform pressure at the interface of the SU-8 well features and 3D printed inserts, sealing the glue between. The clamped assembly is then immersed in acetone to wash out any excess glue that has leaked onto the microchannel features before being put in the oven to heat-cure the glue.

This method allows for integration of large well features on the silicon mold containing fine SU-8 features using low-end resin 3D printers in a reproducible manner without the need of manual placement of printed pieces. Integration of large well features on the mold guarantees the formation of large wells during the casting process, eliminating all need for post-processing of PDMS to create large wells. Since all pieces are aligned at once during the mold fabrication, the assembly time is independent of the number of pieces to be interfaced which opens the possibility for more complex designs to be fabricated rapidly and reliably. Because the well insert pieces can be printed before or during wafer preparation, printing of the pieces does not constitute a bottleneck during mold fabrication. Furthermore, the use of low-viscocity heat-curing glue followed by an acetone wash significantly speeds up and simplifies the application of adhesive. The low viscocity makes the application of sufficient glue rapid and consistent and the heat-curing nature of the glue allows for an easy cleanup of excess glue prior to curing.
