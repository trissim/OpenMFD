# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas, Yonatan Morocz, Jathav Puvirajan, David Juncker, and Alyson Fournier  
**Affiliations:** McGill University  
**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Plate-format devices made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber, can combine spatially controlled cell culture with multichannel pipetting and automated imaging. Pipette-accessible wells are often punched individually after curing, repeating this operation for every opening in every cast. We developed reusable silicon-wafer molds that form both the microfeatures and the wells during casting. The molds combine features patterned in SU-8/SUEX light-sensitive resins with 3D-printed resin inserts. OpenMFD generates the photomasks and insert array from shared device dimensions, placing the printed alignment pins and wafer holes at matching coordinates so that the inserts can be transferred and bonded as one array. Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The tested plate contained 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on the standard 9 mm grid, supporting multichannel pipetting and imaging on unmodified high-content systems. Assays demonstrated directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices, and retrograde axon labeling. Once the mold is made, subsequent batches use benchtop casting and assembly, with all wells formed in each cast.

## Introduction
Microfluidic devices give cell biologists spatial control over cell bodies, axons and reagent exposure, supporting experiments on gradients, migration, differentiation and co-culture interactions. Established compartmentalized culture devices support a range of neuronal experiments (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and commercial devices are available from suppliers including Xona Microfluidics and ANANDA Devices. Arranging devices in a plate format connects this spatial control to multichannel pipetting and automated imaging across many cultures. Academic laboratories developing custom layouts also need a way to fabricate these arrays locally and produce successive batches.

Many academic laboratories make custom devices from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber. Light-based patterning (photolithography) produces a reusable mold; the laboratory then casts the PDMS, removes it from the mold (demolding), and bonds it to glass. For many academic biology groups, access to a shared cleanroom for mold fabrication is more practical than acquiring precision molding or machining equipment. Once the mold is available, subsequent casts can be made and assembled on the bench. Commercial plates and hard-plastic methods based on hot embossing (pressing heated plastic against a mold), computer-controlled milling, or injection molding provide alternatives. Supplementary Table S1 compares the equipment and production steps used by these approaches.

A plate-format mold must combine micrometer-sized channels with millimeter-deep, pipette-accessible wells. SU-8, a light-sensitive resin, reliably forms the channel features, whereas the well openings are commonly punched into the PDMS after curing. Forming the wells in the mold would remove this repeated operation from each cast. Resin 3D printing can produce molds for PDMS casting as well as parts incorporated directly into the finished device; printer, resin, orientation and post-treatment affect printing accuracy and biological compatibility (Meynard et al., 2025; Venzac, 2025). Manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have also been bonded to SU-8 molds to form open compartments around photolithographic microtunnels in neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019).

Our approach combines shared design dimensions with collective insert transfer to make a reusable mold. OpenMFD defines the microfeatures, insert pins and matching SUEX alignment holes in the same coordinate system and applies the same correction for PDMS shrinkage to the masks and inserts. Matching pin and hole positions allow the printed inserts to be aligned with the wafer as one array. A compressible backing accommodates pin-height differences during bonding. The bonded inserts remain in the mold and form every well in subsequent PDMS casts, so repeated device production follows the usual casting and assembly sequence.

In the tested plate, each cast formed all 96 wells across 48 two-compartment devices. Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The assembled devices supported multichannel pipetting, automated imaging and cortical-neuron culture in interior devices. Tracer redistribution and retrograde axon labeling assessed transport and neuronal compatibility. Additional file-generation examples for three layouts examined how matching masks, inserts and frames change together.

## Materials and Methods
Mold fabrication produced the reusable templates; casting and assembly were then repeated to make device arrays for the assays.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
To align all inserts together, we used OpenMFD (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`) to place the insert pins and wafer holes at matching coordinates. The same well, chamber, channel and array dimensions generated the photomasks, insert array, frame, wafer outlines and cutting guides. Device shapes were defined in OpenSCAD design software using the SolidPython library and exported as two-dimensional DXF mask drawings for each layer and three-dimensional STL models of the inserts and frames. Matching solid and hollow marks aligned the photolithographic layers. The insert STL and SUEX photomask used the same coordinates for the pins and holes, connecting file generation directly to collective array alignment.

The tested layout placed 48 two-compartment devices on the standard 9 mm well grid, using a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart. Each device had 62 microchannels with a design width of 10 µm and an interchannel gap of 30 µm. Matching masks and inserts received a horizontal scale factor of 1.0226 (2.26% enlargement) for the 100°C PDMS cure. Supplementary Table S2 gives the nominal device, channel, pin, taper, and frame dimensions used to generate the fabrication files.

To test layout generation beyond the neuronal device, we generated matched photomasks, inserts, wafer outlines and frames for three literature-inspired layouts. These were an oligodendrocyte myelination layout (Kerman et al., 2015), an axon-guidance layout with gradients at right angles to each other (Taylor et al., 2015), and a three-compartment assay (Coquinco et al., 2014). Supplementary Table S2 gives their dimensions and fabrication constraints. These additional layouts were generated but not fabricated or biologically tested.

### Hybrid mold fabrication
To combine fine channels and millimeter-deep wells in one reusable mold, we assembled light-patterned SU-8/SUEX photoresists and printed resin inserts on a 6-inch silicon wafer. Two layers of SU-8 2005 liquid photoresist, each with a 5 µm target thickness, formed an adhesion base and the patterned microchannel features. A 200 µm SUEX K200 dry-film layer defined the well features and insert-alignment holes. We used a flood-exposed base and a bake before SUEX lamination to support microchannel adhesion during later processing. We also filtered the SUEX exposure with an LP360 filter to reduce raised edges at the insert-bonding surface. Supplementary Notes S1 and S2 illustrate the exposure and adhesion observations, respectively.

To preserve insert positions during transfer, we kept the printed array on its detachable, flexible metal build plate. The inserts were printed from Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin on an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro; Supplementary Data S2 provides the complete printing profile. Square pins seated in matching SUEX holes with 75 µm nominal clearance per side, and EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy bonded the array to the wafer. The assembly was clamped for washing and curing (Figure 2C). A compressible EPDM rubber backing accommodated pin-height differences under clamping (Figure 2D). After washing and curing, the carrier was flexed away, leaving the bonded inserts on the wafer.

To separate the printed resin and adhesive from cast PDMS and aid demolding, we coated the bonded mold with 1 µm parylene C (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Design dimensions refer to the mold before parylene coating. Supplementary Protocol S2 provides the complete mold-fabrication procedure.

### PDMS casting and plate assembly
To keep the wells open during casting, we poured Sylgard 184 PDMS below the insert tops. The PDMS was mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio. Filled molds were degassed and cured for 1 h at 100°C; the wafer rack allowed multiple molds to be processed in parallel (Figure 3, steps 1–4). Tapered mold features aided demolding, following the principle used in earlier multiscale molds (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019).

To give the cast an optically accessible base, we trimmed the PDMS array and plasma-bonded it to a coverslip-glass sheet (Figure 3, steps 5–6). Molded cutting guides defined the 108 × 72 mm cast footprint, which fitted the 110 × 74 × 0.17 mm glass sheet. We then autoclaved the bonded assembly before mounting it in a frame (Figure 3, step 7).

To support the glass-bonded array during plate handling, we mounted it in a printed frame (Figure 3, step 8). The frame was printed in high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a rubber-modified plastic. Supplementary Data S3 supplies its printing profile and G-code. LOCTITE SI 5140, a room-temperature-curing silicone sealant, joined the device to the frame (Henkel, 2023).

To prepare the assembled device for aqueous coating and culture, we applied a second dry-air plasma treatment after the frame adhesive had cured (Figure 3, step 9). The earlier plasma treatment activates PDMS and glass for bonding; the second prepares the assembled device's exposed surface. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete casting, assembly and culture procedure.

### Mold and device characterization
To quantify the pin-height differences the backing needed to accommodate, we measured all 96 pins in one printed array with a micrometer. Each pin was measured individually before bonding; the array contained 8 rows and 12 columns. Supplementary Data S1 supplies the measurements and their deviations from the array mean.

To judge whether molds continued to produce usable casts, we inspected the patterned wafers and cast PDMS. We checked that the SU-8/SUEX features remained attached, the inserts stayed seated and bonded, and the cast wells and microchannels were open and intact. Microscopy was used to inspect the channels after coating and casting. Supplementary Table S3 records the numbers of molds and usable casts, elapsed use and the inspection-based definition of a usable cast.

To document the channel geometry visible in cast devices, we read apparent widths between the centres of opposing dark wall bands in brightfield images. Three channel positions were measured in one interior device from each of the three plates, giving nine distances at a native calibration of 1.18675253 µm per pixel. Supplementary Figure S3 shows the placements, and Supplementary Data S4 provides the full measurements.

To select materials for the frame assembly, we visually inspected printed frames and assembled joints. To screen adhesives, we assessed whether primary cortical-neuron cultures survived to the end of the experiment. Cell toxicity was not quantified.

To test whether cells could grow at the outer positions without the frame, one PDMS device was bonded to glass without a frame or frame adhesive and placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish. This control removed both the frame assembly and the sealing effect of the frame adhesive.

To check compatibility with routine plate handling, we fitted eight tips to a 12-channel pipette and filled eight wells in one plate column in parallel. To check imaging compatibility, we focused and collected images on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal microscopes at the Early Drug Discovery Unit (EDDU), The Neuro, McGill University. Neither instrument required custom plate handling or modification.

### Directional tracer-movement assay
To bias movement between the compartments, we loaded unequal reservoir volumes and compared tracer fluorescence immediately after loading and after 72 h in 24 two-compartment devices. Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in PBS was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. The volume difference was established once, and the devices were kept in a cell-culture incubator for 72 h without replenishment or volume adjustment. Images were acquired in widefield mode using a 10× objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.30, 2 × 2 camera binning, a 100 ms exposure, and red and green channels suited to the two fluorophores. Tracer movement was assessed visually by comparing green fluorescence appearing in the initially lower-volume compartment with red fluorescence appearing in the opposite compartment. Flow rate and the duration of the pressure difference were not measured.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
To test neuronal culture in the assembled devices, primary cortical neurons were isolated from one embryonic day 18 (E18) Sprague Dawley rat embryo following Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. Routine culture maintenance followed the cited protocol, with the device-specific conditions described below. The three plates were technical replicates of a single neuronal preparation. Dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences) was selected as the cell-attachment coating based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water. Neurons were seeded into the cell-body (soma) compartment in 50 µL Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 5,000–10,000 cells per well, allowed to attach for at least 30 min, and then switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). Volumes of 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment created a small pressure difference that directed fluid from the soma compartment toward the distal compartment during culture.

For retrograde axon labeling, Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) was applied to the lower-volume distal compartment at 1 µg/mL on day 10 in vitro (DIV10). Cultures were maintained to DIV11. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis was restricted to the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges. Devices were excluded only when contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in the final image; high or low cell counts alone were not grounds for exclusion. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete procedure.

### Imaging and cell counting
To obtain endpoint images for CTB-positive cell-body counts, we acquired widefield images at DIV11 on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus at EDDU using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, and laser autofocus. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm. Brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure; Alexa 647 images used 640 nm excitation, 706 nm emission, and a 100 ms exposure.

To compare counts over the same device area, we aligned and cropped the stitched images despite differences in their original coverage. Images were collected at 35 positions (7 × 5 grid) on Plates 1 and 2 and 42 positions (7 × 6 grid) on Plate 3, with approximately 10% overlap. These images were stitched into one image per device. Each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to the same area for cell counting, regardless of the number of imaging positions. One cell count was recorded per analyzed device. These were baseline images collected before axotomy; Supplementary Protocol S1 describes the dataset. The supplementary files include the microscope's saved settings and image positions for Plate 1 in XML format.

## Results

### Hybrid molds form all 96 wells in a single cast
The hybrid molds formed all 96 well openings during each PDMS cast, eliminating individual well punching. OpenMFD used one set of device dimensions (Figure 1A) to generate single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), matching resin inserts (Figure 1D), and the plate frame (Figure 1E). Matching pins and alignment holes allowed all 96 inserts to be positioned together on the patterned wafer. Figure 2A shows the wafer and insert array before clamping; Figure 2B shows the pin–hole geometry that guides their alignment. The completed mold combined SU-8 microchannels, a SUEX well layer, and millimeter-tall resin inserts. Figure 1F shows the assembled plate-format device.

The patterned wafer had to provide a bonding surface for the inserts while retaining the fine channel features. LP360-filtered SUEX exposure substantially reduced raised edges around the patterned features, giving a flatter surface for the insert–wafer bond. The flood-exposed SU-8 base layer and pre-SUEX bake supported microchannel adhesion through subsequent processing (Supplementary Figures S1–S2).

Collective bonding required all inserts to seat against the wafer despite differences in their height. Measurements of 96 pins in one array gave a mean height of 5.551 mm, a standard deviation of 26.7 µm, and a 116 µm range (Supplementary Data S1). The compressible EPDM backing accommodated this variation while the inserts engaged their matching SUEX holes (Figure 2C–D), allowing the array to be transferred without adjusting each insert separately.

The completed molds retained their SU-8 features and seated, bonded inserts. Figure 2E shows an insert–wafer bond, and Figure 2F shows the patterned SUEX alignment hole used to locate an insert pin. After parylene coating, they released cured PDMS without a silane release treatment (silanization), and microscopy showed open channels in the casts. Nine apparent wall-band-centre distances measured in three cast devices ranged from 10.7 to 13.1 µm (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Data S4).

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
The standard 9 mm well spacing allowed parallel pipetting without custom tip spacing: eight wells were filled together with a multichannel pipette. Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array, and multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. Repeated plate production therefore used the existing mold geometry rather than repeating insert alignment or individual well punching.

HIPS provided frames with less visible warping than polypropylene in our printing trials, consistent with the reported challenges of polypropylene extrusion printing (Spoerk et al., 2020). With LOCTITE SI 5140, the assembled plates supported primary cortical-neuron cultures through the experiment. The adhesive formed a flexible, visibly leak-tight glass-to-HIPS joint that remained intact during repeated incubator transfers.

Culture was poorer at the plate edges in the assembled devices. To examine whether the outer PDMS positions themselves could support neurons, we placed one unframed PDMS-on-glass device in a culture dish. Cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere. This control showed that those positions could support culture in that arrangement, but did not separate the effects of the frame and its adhesive seal.

Both the Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal focused and collected images without custom plate handling or instrument modification. The dye and CTB images shown were collected on the Opera Phenix Plus, which was faster for these experiments.

### Directional tracer redistribution and neuronal retrograde labeling
Green tracer redistributed toward the initially lower-volume compartment after one unequal-volume loading (Figure 4A). Images of 24 two-compartment devices acquired immediately after loading and at 72 h showed green fluorescence entering the left compartment, with little visible red signal in the opposite direction. This redistribution is consistent with the initial volume difference driving tracer movement; the two imaging time points do not establish how long that difference persisted.

CTB-647 added to the distal compartment labeled cell bodies on the other side of the microchannels (Figure 4B). Primary cortical neurons from one E18 rat embryo were distributed across three assembled plates. CTB-647 was added on DIV10, and labeled cell bodies were imaged on DIV11. This labeling is consistent with tracer uptake by axons extending into the distal well and transport back toward their cell bodies.

Biological analysis used the 24 interior devices per plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges, giving 72 devices across three plates. Three devices were excluded because contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in their final images, leaving 69 analyzed devices. Figure 4C shows the mean CTB-positive cell-body count per device and the standard deviation within each plate.

## Discussion
Collective insert transfer makes alignment a wafer-scale operation. Ristola et al. combined SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts for a neuron-oligodendrocyte co-culture device (Ristola et al., 2019). Here, shared pin and hole positions allow all 96 resin inserts to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in a single operation. The bonded inserts remain in the reusable mold and form every well during casting.

The pin–hole geometry and compressible backing address different parts of collective seating. Square pins seated in matching SUEX holes limit sideways movement. Their nominal clearance is 75 µm per side, compared with a 1.275 mm chamber margin on each side of the channel array (Supplementary Table S2). This margin is 17 times the per-side clearance; final alignment also depends on printing accuracy and photolithographic layer alignment. The EPDM rubber backing accommodates differences in pin height during bonding. For an array that fits on one wafer and print platform, the same alignment operation is used regardless of the number or arrangement of inserts.

Recent resin-printing methods use both printed molds and printed parts retained in the finished culture device (Meynard et al., 2025). Post-treatment can improve PDMS curing and release from resin molds, but reproducibly printing features near 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). Combining SU-8 photolithography for the channels with resin printing for the wells accommodates both scales. The resin inserts remain in the parylene-coated mold; the culture chambers themselves are cast in PDMS and bonded to glass.

Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The final process combined flat bonding surfaces, attached SU-8 features, and evenly seated inserts in a reusable mold. Once the mold is complete, laboratories can cast and assemble further devices without repeating cleanroom processing. Parylene coating can be performed locally or by an external service.

Changing device dimensions updates the matching masks, inserts, wafer layout, and frame together. Figure 5 illustrates this coordinated file generation for compartment, gradient, and co-culture layouts. Several dimensions must work together: pin and hole sizes determine alignment clearance, the insert skirt (the rim around its base) provides space for adhesive, and the sidewall slope affects demolding and well capacity. The same 2.26% shrinkage correction is applied to the masks and inserts.

Adjusting the shared dimensions can also account for fabrication effects before the matching files are regenerated. A uniform 1 µm parylene coating would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge while leaving its height relative to the coated base approximately unchanged. The present design dimensions specify the mold before coating. Measurements of coated molds and casts could inform a coating correction alongside the existing PDMS shrinkage correction (Supplementary Table S2).

Improving culture at the plate edges would make more of the cast array available for biological experiments. Biological analysis was restricted to the 24 interior positions of each 48-device plate. The unframed control showed that outer PDMS positions could support neurons, but the cause of poorer edge growth in assembled plates remains unresolved. Frame geometry and sealing can be revised independently of the PDMS device and reusable mold.

The present plate accommodates up to 50 µL per well while retaining tapered walls for demolding, the 9 mm well grid, and a 110 × 74 mm glass sheet (Supplementary Table S2). Larger working volumes can be considered by revising the well geometry and glass footprint together; larger custom-cut glass would increase fabrication costs.

Neuronal culture and retrograde labeling were demonstrated in interior devices from one neuronal preparation, while tracer redistribution was assessed at loading and 72 h. Experiments across independent neuronal preparations and time-resolved transport measurements would extend the biological and fluidic characterization.

## Conclusion
Reusable hybrid molds combine photolithographic microfeatures with collectively aligned resin inserts to form all well openings during PDMS casting. OpenMFD supplies the matching mask and insert coordinates needed for array alignment and bonding. In the demonstrated 48-device plate, each cast formed all 96 wells, removing individual well punching from repeated device production. The assembled plates supported multichannel pipetting, automated imaging and compartmentalized neuronal culture in interior devices. Once the mold is fabricated, subsequent batches use benchtop casting and assembly rather than repeating cleanroom processing. The open-source design tools and fabrication procedure provide a starting point for adapting this approach to other plate-format culture devices.
