# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas¹, Yonatan Morocz², Jathav Puvirajan¹, David Juncker², and Alyson Fournier¹

**Affiliations:** ¹Department of Neurology and Neurosurgery, Montréal Neurological Institute, McGill University, Montréal, Québec, Canada

## Abstract
Microfluidic cell-culture platforms combine fine channels with pipette-accessible wells, but producing these features at different scales can require labor-intensive fabrication and assembly. This challenge is particularly relevant to neuronal assays, which use fine channels to connect spatially separated culture compartments. Here, we present an open-source, hybrid microfabrication strategy for producing reusable silicon-wafer molds. The molds combine channel-forming microfeatures made by light-based patterning (photolithography) with well-forming inserts produced by three-dimensional (3D) printing. OpenMFD, a parametric design framework, generates coordinated photomasks and insert arrays from shared device dimensions. Matching coordinates enable array-scale alignment and simultaneous bonding of millimeter-scale inserts to wafers patterned with the light-sensitive SU-8 and SUEX resins. As an example application, we fabricated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) plates containing 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on a standard 9 mm grid, eliminating individual well punching during device production. The plates supported multichannel liquid handling, automated high-content imaging, directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices and retrograde axonal labeling. OpenMFD also generated matching fabrication files for alternative compartment layouts.

## Introduction
Compartmentalized neuronal cultures enable independent access to cell bodies, axons and their local extracellular environments. Microchannel-connected culture chambers permit spatially restricted treatments, isolation of axonal material and analysis of axonal injury, regeneration and transport (Taylor et al., 2005). Subsequent designs have enabled controlled axon-guidance gradients (Taylor et al., 2015), localized neuron–glia interactions (Park et al., 2012) and modular neuronal culture models (Megarity et al., 2022). The geometry of connecting microchannels also influences device performance and the study of communication between neuronal populations (Goshi et al., 2022).

Extending these experimental capabilities to parallel assays requires platforms that accommodate multiple treatment conditions, biological replicates and established liquid-handling and imaging workflows. A high-capacity microfluidic plate containing up to 96 three-compartment culture units has been used with primary rodent neurons and human neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (Moll et al., 2024). Its functional measurements used pairs of connected compartments rather than that three-compartment configuration. Other approaches prioritizing experimental flexibility include modular microfluidic units that can be combined into customizable neuronal culture configurations (Megarity et al., 2022). Fluid-walled chambers, in which liquid boundaries replace solid walls, support human neuronal networks and moderate-throughput axotomy, or axon-cutting, assays (Nebuloni et al., 2024).

A persistent fabrication challenge in microfluidic cell culture is combining micrometer-scale channels with millimeter-scale, pipette-accessible reservoirs. Conventional soft lithography reproduces fine mold features in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber, but culture reservoirs are often opened individually after casting. Fine channel-forming features, such as the 10 µm widths designed here, can fall below the practical resolution of desktop resin printers. Light-based patterning (photolithography) provides these fine features, while printed structures form the larger wells (Venzac, 2025). This scale mismatch has previously been addressed with hybrid micro–macro molding combining milled (machine-cut) and hot-embossed (heat-pressed) structures to replicate microscopic channels and macroscopic reservoirs without manual punching (Park et al., 2010). The approach was subsequently applied to multi-compartment neuron–glia cultures supporting localized treatments (Park et al., 2012). Other strategies use 3D printing to create compartmentalized neuronal devices without extensive manual post-processing (Kajtez et al., 2020). Supplementary Data S6 compares the equipment and production steps of these fabrication approaches.

For academic laboratories, access to a shared cleanroom for reusable mold fabrication can be more practical than acquiring dedicated precision molding or machining equipment. Light-cured resin 3D printing offers accessible fabrication of larger structures and complex geometries, although printed resins can inhibit PDMS curing and may require additional treatments before use as casting molds (Venzac, 2025). Hybrid assemblies combining soft lithography with printed polymer components provide another route to customizable biological microfluidic systems (Meynard et al., 2025). Parylene C, a thin polymer coating, has also protected printed resins from erosion and prevented cytotoxicity toward primary cells (Musgrove et al., 2023). Ristola and colleagues combined fine features in SU-8, a light-sensitive resin, with individually positioned 3D-printed metal inserts to fabricate co-cultures of neurons and myelin-forming glial cells, or oligodendrocytes (Ristola et al., 2019).

Here, we introduce OpenMFD, an open-source parametric design and hybrid fabrication workflow for producing reusable wafer-scale molds for medium-throughput microfluidic culture devices. The channel and well patterns and the printed inserts must align on the wafer. Changing the well size or spacing therefore requires corresponding changes in the mask drawings and insert array. OpenMFD generates these files and the plate-frame model from one set of dimensions, keeping their positions matched for alignment and bonding of the whole array (Figure 1). Photolithography defines the fine channel-forming features, while the retained printed inserts form the culture wells directly during each PDMS cast, removing individual well punching from successive production runs. We demonstrate this combination of fine channels and pipette-accessible wells in an example neuronal application: an array of 48 two-compartment devices containing 96 wells on a standard 9 mm plate grid. We evaluate repeated casting, compatibility with multichannel pipetting and automated imaging, directional tracer movement, and primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices with retrograde axonal labeling. OpenMFD also arranges alternative compartment geometries into plate-format arrays and generates their matching fabrication files. Once a mold is made, further plates can be cast and assembled on the bench without repeating cleanroom processing.

## Materials and Methods
Mold fabrication produced the reusable templates; casting and assembly were then repeated to make device arrays for the assays.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
To keep the mold and plate components matched, we specified the device geometry and plate layout once in OpenMFD (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`). The input was a Python preset: an editable set of parameters describing the well positions and sizes, channel geometry, device spacing and array size, together with mold dimensions such as insert height and pin–hole clearance (Figure 1A). The software repeated the device across the selected grid and generated the single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), well-forming inserts (Figure 1D) and plate frame (Figure 1E). The mask and insert files shared the positions of the well features, pins and alignment holes, allowing the entire insert array to be aligned with the patterned wafer rather than positioning each insert separately.

OpenMFD converted these parameters into geometric models using the SolidPython library and OpenSCAD design software. Mask layers were exported as two-dimensional DXF drawings for photolithography; inserts and frames were exported as three-dimensional STL models for printing. Wafer outlines and cutting guides were generated from the same dimensions. Matching solid and hollow marks aligned the photolithographic layers, while matching pin and hole positions aligned the printed inserts with the SUEX layer.

The tested layout placed 48 two-compartment devices on the standard 9 mm well grid, using a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart. Each device had 62 microchannels with a design width of 10 µm and an interchannel gap of 30 µm. To compensate for PDMS shrinkage during curing at 100°C, matching masks and inserts were enlarged horizontally by 2.26% (scale factor 1.0226). Supplementary Table S1 gives the nominal device, channel, pin, taper and frame dimensions.

To test layout generation beyond the neuronal device, we generated matched photomasks, inserts, wafer outlines and frames for three literature-inspired layouts. These were an oligodendrocyte myelination layout (Kerman et al., 2015), an axon-guidance layout with gradients at right angles to each other (Taylor et al., 2015), and a three-compartment assay (Coquinco et al., 2014). Supplementary Table S1 gives their dimensions. These additional layouts were generated but not fabricated or biologically tested.

### Hybrid mold fabrication
To combine fine channels and millimeter-deep wells in one reusable mold, we assembled light-patterned SU-8/SUEX photoresists and printed resin inserts on a 6-inch silicon wafer. Two layers of SU-8 2005 liquid photoresist, each with a 5 µm target thickness, formed an adhesion base and the patterned microchannel features. A 200 µm SUEX K200 dry-film layer defined the well features and insert-alignment holes. We exposed the entire SU-8 base layer without a mask and baked the patterned layers before applying the SUEX film to help the fine channel-forming features remain attached during later processing. We also filtered the SUEX exposure with an LP360 filter to reduce raised edges at the insert-bonding surface. Supplementary Notes S1 and S2 illustrate the exposure and adhesion observations, respectively.

To preserve insert positions during transfer, we kept the printed array on its detachable, flexible metal build plate. The inserts were printed from Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin on an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro; Supplementary Data S2 provides the complete printing profile. Square pins seated in matching SUEX holes with 75 µm nominal clearance per side, and EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy bonded the array to the wafer. The assembly was clamped for washing and curing (Figure 2C). A compressible EPDM rubber backing accommodated pin-height differences under clamping (Figure 2D). After washing and curing, the carrier was flexed away, leaving the bonded inserts on the wafer.

To separate the printed resin and adhesive from cast PDMS and aid demolding, we coated the bonded mold with 1 µm parylene C (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Design dimensions refer to the mold before parylene coating. Supplementary Protocol S2 provides the complete mold-fabrication procedure.

### PDMS casting and plate assembly
To keep the wells open during casting, we poured Sylgard 184 PDMS below the insert tops of the coated mold (Figure 3, steps 1–2). The PDMS was mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio. Filled molds were degassed in the wafer rack (Figure 3, step 3) and cured for 1 h at 100°C (Figure 3, step 4); the rack allowed multiple molds to be processed in parallel. Tapered mold features aided demolding, following the principle used in earlier multiscale molds (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019).

To prepare the cast for glass bonding, we demolded and trimmed the PDMS array (Figure 3, steps 5–6). Molded cutting guides defined the 108 × 72 mm cast footprint, which fitted the 110 × 74 × 0.17 mm glass sheet. To give the cast an optically accessible base, we plasma-bonded the trimmed array to the glass. We checked that the PDMS and glass were sealed before autoclaving the bonded assembly (Figure 3, step 7).

To support the glass-bonded array during plate handling, we mounted it in a printed frame (Figure 3, step 8). The frame was printed in high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a rubber-modified plastic. Supplementary Data S3 supplies its printing profile and G-code. LOCTITE SI 5140, a room-temperature-curing silicone sealant, joined the device to the frame (Henkel, 2023).

After the frame adhesive had cured, we applied a second dry-air plasma treatment to make the exposed device surfaces hydrophilic for aqueous coating and culture (Figure 3, step 9). The earlier plasma treatment activated the PDMS and glass surfaces before bonding. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete casting, assembly and culture procedure.

### Mold and device characterization
To quantify the pin-height differences the backing needed to accommodate, we measured all 96 pins in one printed array with a micrometer. Each pin was measured individually before bonding; the array contained 8 rows and 12 columns. Supplementary Data S1 supplies the measurements and their deviations from the array mean.

To judge whether molds continued to produce usable casts, we inspected the patterned wafers and cast PDMS. We checked that the SU-8/SUEX features remained attached, the inserts stayed seated and bonded, and the cast wells and microchannels were open and intact. Microscopy was used to inspect the channels after coating and casting. The inspection criteria and mold-use outcomes are described at the end of Supplementary Protocol S2.

To document the channel geometry visible in cast devices, we read apparent widths between the centres of opposing dark wall bands in brightfield images. Three channel positions were measured in one interior device from each of the three plates, giving nine distances at a native calibration of 1.18675253 µm per pixel. Supplementary Figure S3 shows the placements, and Supplementary Data S4 provides the full measurements.

We inspected the printed frames for warping and checked that their surfaces were flat throughout. To screen adhesives, we assessed whether primary cortical-neuron cultures survived to the end of the experiment. Cell toxicity was not quantified.

To test whether cells could grow at the outer positions without the frame, one PDMS device was bonded to glass without a frame or frame adhesive and placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish. This control removed both the frame assembly and the sealing effect of the frame adhesive.

To check compatibility with routine plate handling, we fitted eight tips to a 12-channel pipette and filled eight wells in one plate column in parallel. To check imaging compatibility, we focused and collected images on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal microscopes at the Early Drug Discovery Unit (EDDU), The Neuro, McGill University. Neither instrument required custom plate handling or modification.

### Directional tracer-movement assay
To bias movement between the compartments, we loaded unequal reservoir volumes and compared tracer fluorescence immediately after loading and after 72 h in 24 two-compartment devices. Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in PBS was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. The volume difference was established once, and the devices were kept in a cell-culture incubator for 72 h without replenishment or volume adjustment. Images were acquired in widefield mode using a 10× objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.30, 2 × 2 camera binning, a 100 ms exposure, and red and green channels suited to the two fluorophores. Tracer movement was assessed visually by comparing green fluorescence appearing in the initially lower-volume compartment with red fluorescence appearing in the opposite compartment. Flow rate and the duration of the pressure difference were not measured.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
To test whether the assembled devices supported neuronal culture and axonal growth, primary cortical neurons were isolated from one embryonic day 18 (E18) Sprague Dawley rat embryo following Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. Routine culture maintenance followed the cited protocol, with the device-specific conditions described below. The three plates were technical replicates of a single neuronal preparation.

To promote neuronal attachment, we coated the wells with dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences), selected based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water.

We seeded the cell-body (soma) compartment with 5,000–10,000 neurons in 50 µL medium per well. The seeding medium was Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After at least 30 min for attachment, we switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). To bias fluid movement toward the distal compartment, on the opposite side of the microchannels, we used 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment.

To test whether the cultured neurons extended axons into the distal compartment, we applied Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) to that compartment. The tracer was added at 1 µg/mL on day 10 in vitro (DIV10). Cultures were maintained to DIV11. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis was restricted to the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges. Five devices were excluded from the cell-count analysis because of segmentation or cell-count artifacts. The exclusions are identified in Supplementary Data S5; cell-count value alone was not an exclusion criterion. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete procedure.

### Imaging and cell counting
We acquired widefield images of CTB-labeled cell bodies in each device at DIV11 on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus at EDDU using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, and laser autofocus. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm. Brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure; Alexa 647 images used 640 nm excitation, 706 nm emission, and a 100 ms exposure.

To compare counts over the same device area, we aligned and cropped the stitched images despite differences in their original coverage. Images were collected at 35 positions (7 × 5 grid) on Plates 1 and 2 and 42 positions (7 × 6 grid) on Plate 3, with approximately 10% overlap. These images were stitched into one image per device. Each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to the same area for cell counting, regardless of the number of imaging positions. One cell count was recorded per analyzed device. These were baseline images collected before axotomy; Supplementary Protocol S1 describes the dataset. The supplementary files include the microscope's saved settings and image positions for Plate 1 in XML format.

## Results

### Hybrid molds form all 96 wells in a single cast
The hybrid molds formed all 96 well openings during each PDMS cast, eliminating individual well punching. One set of device dimensions in OpenMFD (Figure 1A) generated the single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), resin-insert models (Figure 1D) and plate frame (Figure 1E). Each printed insert pin matched an alignment hole on the patterned wafer, allowing all 96 inserts to be positioned together. Figure 2A shows the wafer and insert array before clamping, and Figure 2B shows the pin–hole fit. The completed mold combined fine SU-8 channel-forming features, a SUEX well layer and millimeter-tall resin inserts. Casting and assembling the PDMS array produced the plate-format device shown in Figure 1F.

LP360-filtered exposure substantially reduced raised edges around the SUEX features, producing a flatter surface for bonding the inserts. The flood-exposed SU-8 base layer and the bake before SUEX lamination helped the fine channel-forming features remain attached through subsequent processing. Supplementary Figures S1 and S2 illustrate the exposure and adhesion observations, respectively.

Compressible backing allowed the insert array to seat against the wafer despite differences in pin height. Measurements of all 96 pins in one array gave a mean height of 5.551 mm, a standard deviation of 26.7 µm and a range of 116 µm. Rigid clamping did not provide uniform contact across the array. The EPDM rubber backing compressed to accommodate the height differences while the pins seated in their matching SUEX holes (Figure 2C–D), so individual inserts did not need separate adjustment during transfer. Supplementary Data S1 supplies the pin-height measurements.

The completed molds retained their SU-8 features and seated, bonded inserts. Figure 2E shows an insert–wafer bond, and Figure 2F shows the SUEX alignment hole that locates an insert pin. After parylene coating, the molds released cured PDMS without a silane release treatment (silanization), and microscopy showed open channels in the casts. Distances between the centres of opposing dark bands at the channel walls ranged from 10.7 to 13.1 µm across nine measurements in three cast devices. Supplementary Figure S3 shows the measurement positions, and Supplementary Data S4 supplies all nine apparent-width measurements.

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
The standard 9 mm well spacing allowed parallel pipetting without custom tip spacing: eight wells were filled together with a multichannel pipette. Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array, and multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. Repeated plate production therefore used the existing mold geometry rather than repeating insert alignment or individual well punching.

HIPS frames joined to the glass-bonded PDMS arrays with LOCTITE SI 5140 formed flexible joints that remained intact during repeated incubator transfers. HIPS frames showed less visible warping than polypropylene frames in the printing trials, consistent with the reported challenges of polypropylene extrusion printing (Spoerk et al., 2020). LOCTITE SI 5140 was the only screened adhesive that supported primary cortical-neuron cultures through the experimental endpoint.

Culture was poorer at the edges of the framed plates. In one unframed PDMS-on-glass device placed in a culture dish, cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere. The outer PDMS positions could therefore support culture in that arrangement. Removing the frame also removed its adhesive seal and changed the culture vessel, so the comparison did not isolate the cause of poorer edge growth.

Both the Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal focused and collected images without custom plate handling or instrument modification. The dye and CTB images shown were collected on the Opera Phenix Plus, which was faster for these experiments.

### Directional tracer redistribution and neuronal culture
Green tracer redistributed toward the initially lower-volume compartment after one unequal-volume loading (Figure 4A). Images of 24 two-compartment devices acquired immediately after loading and at 72 h showed green fluorescence entering the left compartment, with little visible red signal in the opposite direction. This redistribution is consistent with the initial volume difference driving tracer movement; the two imaging time points do not establish how long that difference persisted.

Cortical neurons grew in the assembled plates and could be labeled from the distal compartment (Figure 4B). Primary cortical neurons from one E18 rat embryo were distributed across three assembled plates. CTB-647 was added to the distal compartment on DIV10, and labeled cell bodies were imaged on DIV11. This labeling is consistent with tracer uptake by axons extending into the distal well and transport back toward their cell bodies.

Cell-count analysis covered the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the edges, giving 72 devices across three plates. Five devices were excluded because of segmentation or cell-count artifacts, leaving 67 analyzed devices: 22 from Plate 1, 21 from Plate 2 and 24 from Plate 3. Figure 4C summarizes the CTB-positive cell-body counts for each plate.

## Discussion
Reusable hybrid molds separate one-time insert alignment from repeated plate production. Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. Once the well-forming inserts are aligned and bonded, each cast forms all well openings from the same assembly. Further plates can therefore be made by benchtop casting and assembly without repeating cleanroom processing. Supplementary Protocol S2 describes mold inspection, reuse and casting failures.

Combining photolithography with resin printing allows fine channel features and larger well shapes to be formed in the same mold. Resin-printing methods can produce casting molds or parts that remain in the finished culture device (Meynard et al., 2025). Treating the resin can improve PDMS curing and release, but reliably printing features near 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). In the hybrid mold, photolithography forms the fine features and the printed inserts form the wells. Parylene C coats the mold surface, and the inserts remain bonded for subsequent casts; the culture chambers are made of PDMS bonded to glass. The coating can be applied locally or by an external service.

Collective transfer extends insert alignment from individual pieces to a complete array. Ristola et al. (2019) combined fine SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts in a device for culturing neurons with myelin-forming glial cells, or oligodendrocytes. Shared pin and hole coordinates allowed all 96 resin inserts in the neuronal plate mold to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in one operation. The square pins constrain sideways movement in the patterned SUEX holes, while the EPDM rubber backing accommodates differences in pin height. These two functions let the inserts align and seat together. For layouts that fit on one wafer and print platform, the alignment operation remains the same as the insert number or arrangement changes.

The design places a 1.275 mm chamber margin beside each side of the channel array, compared with a nominal pin–hole clearance of 75 µm per side. The margin is 17 times that clearance. Both distances come from the design; actual alignment also depends on printing accuracy and alignment of the photolithographic layers. The insert's adhesive-contact rim, or skirt, provides the region for bonding to the wafer. Supplementary Table S1 gives the pin and hole dimensions, chamber margin and adhesive-contact geometry.

Generating the masks and inserts from shared dimensions keeps their designed positions matched when a layout changes. The same changes update the wafer outline and frame. The neuronal layout was fabricated and used for culture; the three additional sets of photomasks and insert models in Figure 5 were generated as fabrication files only.

The size and shape of the wells link working volume to the plate dimensions. The neuronal plate accommodates up to 50 µL per well on a 9 mm well grid over a 110 × 74 mm glass sheet. Its sloped, or tapered, walls help the cured PDMS release from the mold but also affect well capacity. Larger working volumes require considering well geometry and the supporting glass-sheet dimensions together; larger custom-cut glass would increase fabrication costs. Supplementary Table S1 gives the well, taper and glass-sheet dimensions.

Shrinkage compensation and parylene coating change dimensions at different stages of fabrication. The masks and inserts receive the same 2.26% horizontal enlargement to compensate for PDMS shrinkage during curing. The nominal mold dimensions describe the features before coating. A uniform 1 µm parylene film would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge because both sides receive the film. Its height relative to the coated base would remain approximately unchanged because the ridge and base would both be coated. Supplementary Table S1 gives the shrinkage compensation and the calculated effect of a uniform coating.

Brightfield images provided apparent widths from the spacing of the visible channel-wall bands. The nine distances from three devices, measured between the centres of opposing dark bands, ranged from 10.7 to 13.1 µm. At the recorded calibration, moving each endpoint by one pixel can change a span by up to 2.37 µm. This change describes sensitivity to selecting the band centres; it does not bound physical measurement error. The images show lateral band spacing and do not determine channel depth or the full cross-sectional profile. Supplementary Figure S3 shows the measurement positions, and Supplementary Data S4 provides all nine distances, calibration and pixel coordinates.

Tracer redistribution was consistent with movement toward the initially lower-volume compartment. Images at loading and 72 h documented the change after one unequal-volume loading, but did not establish how long the initial volume difference persisted.

The assembled plates supported routine handling, imaging and retrograde axon labeling in interior neuronal cultures. Compartmentalized neuronal cultures allow axons to be treated separately from their cell bodies. CTB applied to the distal compartment has been used to identify neurons whose axons reach it in studies of neuronal signaling and disease (Maimon et al., 2021; Moya-Alvarado et al., 2023).

Three plates received cortical neurons from one embryonic day 18 (E18) rat embryo, making them technical replicates of a single preparation. Analysis covered the 24 interior positions of each 48-device plate because culture was poorer at the edges. Neurons grew at outer PDMS positions in one unframed control, showing that those positions could support culture in that arrangement. The control also removed the adhesive seal and changed the culture vessel, so it did not isolate the cause of poorer edge growth. Frame geometry and sealing can be revised independently of the PDMS device and reusable mold.

## Conclusion
Combining photolithography with collectively aligned resin inserts moves well formation into the reusable mold. In the 48-device neuronal plate, each PDMS cast formed all 96 wells, removing individual well punching from repeated production. OpenMFD generates the matching photomasks and insert models needed to align and bond the array. The assembled plates supported multichannel pipetting, automated imaging and compartmentalized neuronal culture in interior devices. Subsequent plates can be cast and assembled on the bench without repeating cleanroom processing. The open-source software also generates coordinated fabrication files for alternative compartment layouts.
