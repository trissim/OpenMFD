# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas, Yonatan Morocz, Jathav Puvirajan, David Juncker, and Alyson Fournier  
**Affiliations:** McGill University  
**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Plate-format devices made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber, can combine spatially controlled cell culture with multichannel liquid handling and automated imaging, but pipette-accessible wells are often punched individually after curing. We developed a design and fabrication method for reusable silicon-wafer molds that combine microfeatures patterned in SU-8/SUEX light-sensitive resins with 3D-printed resin inserts to form all wells during casting. OpenMFD generates the photomasks and insert array from shared device dimensions so that the printed pins match the alignment-hole positions on the wafer. The inserts are then transferred and bonded as one array. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The tested plate contained 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on the standard 9 mm grid, supporting multichannel pipetting and imaging on unmodified high-content systems. Assays demonstrated directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture, and retrograde axon labeling. Culture validation was limited to interior devices because growth was poorer at the plate edges. Once the mold is made, subsequent batches use benchtop casting and assembly, with all wells formed in each cast.

## Introduction
Microfluidic devices give cell biologists spatial control over cell bodies, axons, local reagent exposure, gradients, migration, differentiation, and co-culture interactions. Established designs support applications across neuroscience, stem cell biology, cancer research, immunology, and developmental biology (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and commercial devices are available from suppliers including Xona Microfluidics and ANANDA Devices. Plate-format devices extend these capabilities to experiments with many replicates using multichannel pipettes and automated imaging. For academic laboratories, custom designs and affordable local fabrication are important.

Experiments with many biological replicates or treatment groups benefit from devices arranged for standard pipetting and long-term culture. Multiwell plates provide this format. Many academic laboratories make custom devices from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber. They use light-based patterning (photolithography) to fabricate a reusable mold, then cast the devices, remove them from the mold (demolding), and bond them to glass. Commercial plates and hard-plastic methods based on hot embossing (pressing heated plastic against a mold), computer-controlled milling, or injection molding provide alternatives. For many academic biology groups, having a shared cleanroom fabricate a reusable mold is more accessible than acquiring precision molding or machining equipment. The laboratory can then cast and assemble devices on the bench. Hard-plastic methods require specialized equipment during each production run and may still require photolithography or another high-resolution process to define the micrometer-scale channels (Supplementary Table S2).

The fabrication challenge is the difference in scale between micrometer-sized channels and millimeter-deep wells. Patterning the light-sensitive resin SU-8 reliably forms the channels, whereas pipette-accessible openings are commonly punched into the PDMS after curing. Laser removal of material (ablation), direct printing, hot embossing, hard-plastic fabrication, and modular inserts combine these scales using different equipment and added operations (Supplementary Table S2). Resin 3D printing can produce molds for PDMS casting as well as parts incorporated directly into the finished device. The choice of printer, resin, print orientation, and post-treatment affects printing accuracy and biological compatibility (Meynard et al., 2025; Venzac, 2025). Manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have also been bonded to SU-8 molds to form open compartments around photolithographic microtunnels in neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019).

Our approach combines shared design dimensions with collective insert transfer to make a reusable mold. OpenMFD defines the microfeatures, insert pins, and matching SUEX alignment holes in the same coordinate system and applies the same correction for PDMS shrinkage to the masks and inserts. The matching positions allow the printed inserts to be aligned with the wafer as one array. A compressible backing accommodates pin-height differences during bonding. The bonded inserts remain in the mold and form every well in subsequent PDMS casts, so each batch follows the usual casting and assembly sequence.

We evaluated the method using a compartmentalized neuronal culture device, from file generation and mold fabrication through repeated casting, plate handling, imaging, tracer-movement testing, and long-term culture. We also examined how the same design method generates matching masks, inserts, and frames for three literature-inspired layouts.

## Materials and Methods
The two-compartment design was fabricated and evaluated through casting, assembly, directional tracer movement, neuronal culture, and imaging. Supplementary Protocol S1 describes routine device production and assays; Supplementary Protocol S2 describes one-time hybrid mold fabrication.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
OpenMFD (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`) generated photomasks, insert arrays, wafer outlines, cutting guides, and plate frames from shared well, chamber, channel, and array dimensions (Figure 1). Device shapes were defined in OpenSCAD design software using the SolidPython library and exported as two-dimensional DXF mask drawings for each layer and three-dimensional STL models of the inserts and frames. Matching solid and hollow marks were used to align the photolithographic layers. The pin positions in the insert STL and the hole positions in the SUEX photomask came from the same coordinates, allowing the printed array to be aligned with the patterned wafer as a unit.

The tested design contained a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart, giving 48 devices and 96 wells on a 9 mm grid. Each device had 62 microchannels with a design width of 10 µm and an interchannel gap of 30 µm. Matching masks and inserts received a horizontal scale factor of 1.0226 (2.26% enlargement) for the 100°C PDMS cure. Supplementary Table S3 gives the nominal device, channel, pin, taper, and frame dimensions used to generate the fabrication files.

OpenMFD also generated matched photomasks, inserts, wafer outlines, and frames for three literature-inspired layouts: oligodendrocyte myelination (Kerman et al., 2015), axon guidance with gradients at right angles to each other (Taylor et al., 2015), and a three-compartment assay (Coquinco et al., 2014). These devices were not fabricated or biologically tested (Supplementary Table S3).

### Hybrid mold fabrication
The mold comprised two 5 µm layers of SU-8 2005 liquid photoresist (Kayaku Advanced Materials), a 200 µm layer of SUEX K200 dry-film photoresist (DJ MicroLaminates), and a printed resin-insert array on a 6-inch silicon wafer (UniversityWafer ID857). These photoresists are light-sensitive resins used to pattern the mold features. SU-8 was spread by spinning the wafer (spin-coating) using a Laurell WS-650-8B, SUEX was laminated using an SK 335R6, and photolithographic layers were aligned and exposed using an EVG620. Unless otherwise noted, materials were processed according to the manufacturers' instructions; the SU-8 2005 procedure targeted a 5 µm layer. Changes to the manufacturers' procedures are described below and in Supplementary Protocol S2. No dehydration bake or HMDS adhesion-promoter treatment was used. Facility-supplied propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) was the developer solvent used to remove unexposed material from both photoresists.

**Layer 1 (base):** SU-8 2005 was spin-coated to a 5 µm target thickness, flood-exposed without a mask, and left undeveloped beneath the patterned microchannel layer (Supplementary Note S2).

**Layer 2 (microchannels):** A second 5 µm SU-8 2005 layer was exposed through the microchannel photomask and developed. Before SUEX lamination, the wafer bearing the approximately 10 µm SU-8 stack was placed directly in an oven at 150°C for 10 min, then removed without controlled heating or cooling.

**Layer 3 (wells):** SUEX K200 dry film (200 µm) defined the well features and insert-alignment holes. The SUEX was exposed at 2800 mJ/cm² through an LP360 long-pass optical filter to block shorter-wavelength ultraviolet light. After exposure, the oven was heated from room temperature to 50°C at 1°C/min, held at 50°C for at least 12 h overnight, then switched off and allowed to cool. The wafer was submerged in developer and mixed for 20 min; the developer was replaced at 15 min. For the hard bake, the oven was heated to 120°C at 3°C/min, held for 30 min, then switched off and allowed to cool.

Well inserts were printed at their final relative positions using an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro and Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin. The profile used 20 µm layers, 5.5 s normal exposure, 50 s bottom exposure, 7 bottom layers, edge smoothing (anti-aliasing) at grey level 2, and no shrinkage or tolerance compensation in the print-preparation software. Supplementary Table S4 provides the remaining motion, timing, washing, and UV post-cure settings. Each insert had a 1.85 × 1.85 mm alignment pin corresponding to a 2.0 × 2.0 mm SUEX hole, giving 150 µm total clearance (75 µm per side).

The inserts were kept on a detachable flexible metal build plate so the whole array could be transferred at once. EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy adhesive was applied around each pin, and the mold was positioned until the pins seated in their SUEX holes. An EPDM rubber sheet (0.03125 inch thick; Shore A hardness 60) between the fixed magnet and removable build plate provided a compressible backing; a second flat plate and clamps distributed pressure across the assembly (Figure 2). The clamped assembly was washed in two successive 1 min acetone baths, thoroughly blow-dried, and cured at room temperature for 48 h. After curing, the assembly was cleaned in an ultrasonic acetone bath for 10 min to remove residual material. The flexible metal build plate was flexed to release the bonded array (Supplementary Protocol S2).

After bonding, the hybrid mold received a 1 µm coating of the polymer parylene C to separate the printed resin and adhesive from the cast PDMS and aid release during demolding (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Parylene was deposited using a Specialty Coating Systems SCS 200 coater at the McGill Nanotools Microfab facility. The specified channel dimensions are the design dimensions before parylene coating.

### PDMS casting and plate assembly
Frames were generated with the other fabrication files and printed in high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a rubber-modified plastic, on an enclosed Creality K1C using a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.20 mm layers, a 220°C nozzle, and a 100°C build plate. The remaining printing settings are listed in Supplementary Table S6 and included in the supplied Creality Print 6.0.4 G-code printer-instruction file.

PDMS (Sylgard 184) was mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio and poured over the full wafer within an aluminum-foil reservoir. The fill level remained below the insert tops to leave the wells open. Filled molds were supported in a six-shelf metal wafer rack, degassed until visible bubbles were removed (typically 10–30 min; approximately −15 inHg in our setup), and cured for 1 h at 100°C. Multiple molds could be degassed and cured in parallel.

The large mold features had tapered sidewalls to aid demolding, following the same design principle used in earlier multiscale PDMS molds (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019). The wafer was supported on a flat surface, and peeling proceeded from the corners toward the center while the PDMS was pulled backward to reduce local stress on the wafer.

A rectangular guide molded around the array was aligned to an industrial paper guillotine to trim each cast to 108 × 72 mm. The trimmed PDMS and a 110 × 74 × 0.17 mm coverslip-glass sheet were activated with dry-air plasma for 1 min at 200–600 mTorr in a Harrick PDC-001, brought into contact, and heated at 100°C for at least 1 min under uniform pressure (Supplementary Protocol S1).

The bonded PDMS-on-glass assembly was autoclaved in a sterilization pouch at 121°C for 15 min before frame assembly. The frame was decontaminated with 70% ethanol and inverted so that its adhesive groove faced upward. Henkel LOCTITE SI 5140 (3 oz tube, IDH 135264), a one-part silicone sealant that cures at room temperature, was dispensed into the groove (typically 0.5–1 mL per frame) (Henkel, 2023). The bonded device was inverted with the glass facing upward and seated into the frame. After a 3-day room-temperature cure, the assembly was turned upright and excess adhesive was trimmed with a razor blade.

After adhesive curing, the assembled device received a second dry-air plasma treatment (Harrick PDC-001; high power, 30 W, 10 min, 400 mTorr) before aqueous coating and culture. This surface-treatment step was distinct from the earlier 1-min PDMS-glass bonding treatment.

### Mold and device characterization
Each pin height was measured individually with a micrometer at all 96 positions of one 8 × 12 printed array before bonding. Supplementary Table S1 reports the individual values, mean, standard deviation, and difference between the tallest and shortest pins. Molds and casts were inspected to check that the SU-8/SUEX features remained attached, the inserts were seated and bonded, and the cast wells and microchannels were open and intact. Microscopy was used to inspect the channels after coating and casting. Supplementary Table S5 reports mold numbers and usable-cast yield separately and defines a usable cast.

Printed frames and assembled joints were inspected visually while selecting frame materials. Frame adhesives were screened by whether primary cortical-neuron cultures survived to the end of the experiment. Cell toxicity was not quantified.

To test whether cells could grow at the outer positions without the frame, one PDMS device was bonded to glass without a frame or frame adhesive and placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish. This control removed both the frame assembly and the sealing effect of the frame adhesive.

Eight tips were fitted to a 12-channel pipette and aligned with one plate column to fill eight wells in parallel. Compatibility with plate-format imaging was tested by focusing and collecting images on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal microscopes at the Early Drug Discovery Unit (EDDU), The Neuro, McGill University, without custom plate handling or instrument modification.

### Directional tracer-movement assay
Tracer distributions were compared immediately after loading and after 72 h in 24 two-compartment devices. Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in PBS was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. The volume difference was established once, and the devices were kept in a cell-culture incubator for 72 h without replenishment or volume adjustment. Images were acquired in widefield mode using a 10× objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.30, 2 × 2 camera binning, a 100 ms exposure, and red and green channels suited to the two fluorophores. Tracer movement was assessed visually by comparing green fluorescence appearing in the initially lower-volume compartment with red fluorescence appearing in the opposite compartment. Flow rate and the duration of the pressure difference were not measured.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
Primary cortical neurons were isolated from one embryonic day 18 (E18) Sprague Dawley rat embryo following Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. Routine culture maintenance followed the cited protocol, with the device-specific conditions described below. The three plates were technical replicates of a single neuronal preparation. Dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences) was selected as the cell-attachment coating based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water. Neurons were seeded into the cell-body (soma) compartment in 50 µL Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 5,000–10,000 cells per well, allowed to attach for at least 30 min, and then switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). Volumes of 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment created a small pressure difference that directed fluid from the soma compartment toward the distal compartment during culture.

Cultures were maintained for 11 days in vitro (DIV11). At DIV10, Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) was applied to the lower-volume distal compartment at 1 µg/mL. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis was restricted to the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges. Devices were excluded only when contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in the final image; high or low cell counts alone were not grounds for exclusion. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete procedure.

### Imaging and cell counting
CTB images were collected at DIV11 in widefield mode on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus at EDDU using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, and laser autofocus. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm. Brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure; Alexa 647 images used 640 nm excitation, 706 nm emission, and a 100 ms exposure.

For each device, images were collected at 35 positions (7 × 5 grid) on Plates 1 and 2 and 42 positions (7 × 6 grid) on Plate 3, with approximately 10% overlap. These images were stitched into one image per device. The PDMS array on Plate 3 was shifted slightly rightward during gluing, so a larger area was imaged to capture more microchannel exits. Each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to the same area for cell counting, regardless of the number of imaging positions. One cell count was recorded per analyzed device. These were baseline images collected before axotomy; Supplementary Protocol S1 describes the dataset. The supplementary files include the microscope's saved settings and image positions for Plate 1 in XML format.

## Results

### Hybrid molds form all 96 wells in a single cast
OpenMFD generated the photomasks and printed insert array from shared device dimensions, keeping the microfeatures and wells aligned (Figure 1). Matching pins and alignment holes allowed all 96 inserts to be positioned together on the patterned wafer. The completed mold combined SU-8 microchannels, a SUEX well layer, and millimeter-tall resin inserts. During PDMS casting, the inserts formed all 96 wells at once, eliminating individual well punching.

(F) Assembled plate-format device.](figures/rendered/openmfd_design.pdf)

Flat SUEX surfaces were needed for the inserts to form a continuous epoxy seal. Exposure without an LP360 long-pass filter produced raised edges that interfered with sealing. Filtered exposure substantially reduced these ridges and provided a flatter bonding surface (Supplementary Note S1).

The thin SU-8 features also needed to remain attached through SUEX processing and insert bonding. Microchannel features patterned directly on bare silicon detached from the wafer. An SU-8 base layer exposed uniformly without a mask prevented this observed failure. A high-temperature bake before SUEX lamination further improved layer adhesion. In an eight-wafer development batch without this bake, some wafers showed warping and layer separation around the well features during cooling after SUEX processing. Both wafers in a subsequent batch with the 150°C, 10 min bake remained intact (Supplementary Note S2).

Collective bonding required contact across the entire insert array despite differences in pin height. The measured height range was 116 µm across one 8 × 12 array (Supplementary Table S1). With rigid clamping, pressure was uneven across the array. A compressible EPDM rubber sheet backing accommodated the height differences and allowed all pins to seat together. Slow heating at 1–3°C/min during SUEX processing, release from a flexible metal build plate, and room-temperature epoxy curing were used to reduce wafer stress and insert detachment.

With the final process, bonding surfaces were visibly flat, SU-8 features remained attached, and inserts seated in their alignment holes. The parylene-coated molds released cured PDMS without a silane release treatment (silanization). Microscopy showed open channels in the resulting casts. Channel dimensions are reported as nominal values before parylene coating. Dimensional accuracy in the casts remains to be measured.

Five wafer-scale hybrid molds were assembled using the final protocol. Three entered routine use and together produced more than 50 usable casts. One of the other wafers broke during demolding, and leaked PDMS cured beneath the other in the casting rack. Supplementary Table S5 defines a usable cast and reports the mold and cast outcomes. The most recent molds remained in active use for more than 8 months without visible degradation.

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array. Multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. The 96 wells follow the standard 9 mm grid. Eight wells were filled together with a multichannel pipette.

HIPS and polypropylene plastics were evaluated as frame materials. HIPS showed less visible warping in our printing trials and was selected for assembly. Polypropylene warpage during extrusion printing has been described previously (Spoerk et al., 2020).

LOCTITE SI 5140 was the only frame adhesive tested that supported primary cortical-neuron cultures to the end of the experiment; cultures did not survive with any of the other screened adhesives. SI 5140 also formed a flexible, visibly leak-tight glass-to-HIPS joint that remained intact during repeated incubator transfers.

In the single unframed PDMS-on-glass control in a 14.5 cm polystyrene dish, cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere in the device. This observation shows that the outer positions can support culture in the unframed arrangement. The separate contributions of frame geometry, adhesive, sealing, and the surrounding dish environment remain unresolved.

Both the Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal focused and collected images without custom plate handling or instrument modification. The dye and CTB images shown were collected on the Opera Phenix Plus, which was faster for these experiments.

Images of 24 two-compartment devices acquired immediately after loading and at 72 h showed green tracer redistribution toward the initially lower-volume left compartment, with little visible red signal in the opposite direction (Figure 4A). The observed redistribution is consistent with the initial volume difference driving tracer movement. Flow duration and compartmental isolation between the two imaging time points remain uncharacterized.

### Neuronal compatibility demonstrated by CTB retrograde axon tracing
Primary cortical neurons isolated from one E18 rat embryo were distributed across three assembled plates and cultured to DIV11. CTB-647 added to the distal compartment on DIV10 labeled neuronal cell bodies in the soma compartment, consistent with tracer uptake by axons extending into the distal well and retrograde transport.

Biological analysis used the 24 interior devices per plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges, giving 72 devices across three plates. Three devices were excluded because contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in their final images, leaving 69 analyzed devices. ## Discussion
The method brings shared-coordinate design and collective insert bonding together to form a reusable mold. Ristola et al. combined SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts for a neuron-oligodendrocyte co-culture device (Ristola et al., 2019). Here, matching pin and hole positions allow all 96 resin inserts to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in a single operation. The square pins seat in matching SUEX holes, which limit sideways movement. Their nominal clearance is 75 µm per side, compared with a 1.275 mm chamber margin on each side of the channel array (Supplementary Table S3). This margin is 17 times the per-side clearance; final alignment also depends on printing accuracy and photolithographic layer alignment. For an array that fits on one wafer and print platform, the same alignment operation is used regardless of the number or arrangement of inserts. The compressible EPDM rubber sheet backing accommodates pin-height differences during collective bonding. The resulting mold forms every well during casting.

Recent resin-printing methods use both printed molds and printed parts retained in the finished culture device (Meynard et al., 2025). Post-treatment can improve PDMS curing and release from resin molds, but reproducibly printing features near 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). Combining SU-8 photolithography for the channels with resin printing for the wells accommodates both scales. Here, the resin inserts remain in the parylene-coated mold, and each finished culture device consists of PDMS bonded to glass.

Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. Reuse depended on flat bonding surfaces, attached SU-8 features, and evenly seated inserts. Filtered SUEX exposure, the SU-8 base layer and hard bake, compressible clamping layers, and the flexible transfer plate addressed the failures observed during fabrication. Once the mold is complete, laboratories can cast and assemble further devices without repeating cleanroom processing. Parylene coating can be performed locally or by an external service.

Changing device dimensions updates the matching masks, inserts, wafer layout, and frame together. Figure 5 illustrates this coordinated design for compartment, gradient, and co-culture layouts, with fabrication and assay testing still to follow. Several dimensions must work together: pin and hole sizes determine alignment clearance, the insert skirt (the rim around its base) provides space for adhesive, and the sidewall slope affects demolding and well capacity. The same 2.26% shrinkage correction is applied to the masks and inserts.

Parylene coating also changes the mold geometry. A uniform 1 µm coating would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge, a substantial change for the nominal 10 µm channels. The ridge height relative to the surrounding surface would remain approximately unchanged because the coating raises both the ridge top and the flood-exposed base. This predicts wider molded channels, with final dimensions also affected by PDMS shrinkage. Measuring the coated molds and casts would allow a coating-thickness correction to be added to the design parameters. Supplementary Table S3 summarizes these relationships and the checks needed when adapting the design.

The printed plate frame and its glass-to-frame seal are practical targets for further development. The assembled plates supported multichannel handling, automated imaging, and neuronal culture in the interior devices, while cell growth was poorer at the plate edges. In one unframed PDMS-on-glass device placed in a culture dish, cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere. The cause of the poorer edge growth in the assembled plates remains unresolved. Frame geometry and sealing can be revised independently of the PDMS device and reusable mold.

Well geometry and the selected glass size also constrain the plate design. The tapered sidewalls needed for demolding leave less space for wells than in a conventional 96-well plate. At the fixed 9 mm well spacing, taper and the wall thickness between adjacent wells constrain both well width and depth (Supplementary Table S3). The safe maximum working volume in the present plate was 50 µL per well. Slightly larger wells were possible, but would have required a larger glass sheet to accommodate the array. Custom-cut glass would have substantially increased fabrication costs. Applications requiring more than 50 µL per well would therefore need a revised design that balances well capacity, demolding, plate dimensions, and glass cost.

The current characterization combines repeated casting, routine-use observations, two-time-point tracer imaging, and neuronal culture from one embryo. Further testing across independent neuronal preparations would assess biological reproducibility. Time-resolved transport measurements and standardized durability and toxicity tests would extend the assessment of device performance.

## Conclusion
OpenMFD links the design of matching photomasks and printed inserts to collective array alignment and bonding. The resulting reusable mold forms the microfeatures and wells in each PDMS cast, removing individual well punching from repeated device production. The demonstrated devices combine compartmentalized neuronal culture with multichannel pipetting and automated imaging, although culture performance at the plate edges requires improvement. By separating specialized mold fabrication from repeated device production, this approach allows laboratories to make subsequent batches by benchtop casting and assembly. The open-source design tools and fabrication procedure provide a starting point for adapting this approach to other plate-format culture devices.

## Code and data availability
OpenMFD, including the layout-generation code, photomask DXFs, resin-insert models, plate-frame models, and manuscript source, is available at `https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`. A versioned archive of the reported files will be deposited with the submission.

The Supplementary Information contains Tables S1-S6, Protocols S1-S2, and Notes S1-S2.
