# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas¹, Yonatan Morocz², Jathav Puvirajan¹, David Juncker², and Alyson Fournier¹

**Affiliations:** ¹Department of Neurology and Neurosurgery, Montréal Neurological Institute, McGill University, Montréal, Québec, Canada

²Victor Phillip Dahdaleh Institute of Genomic Medicine, McGill University, Montréal, QC, Canada

**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Multi-well microfluidic culture platforms combine fine channels with pipette-accessible wells, but producing these features at different scales can require labor-intensive fabrication and assembly. In neuronal assays, separate compartments connected by fine channels allow cell bodies and axons to be studied independently. We present an open-source, hybrid microfabrication strategy for producing reusable silicon-wafer molds. The molds combine SU-8 photolithography for channel-forming microfeatures with well-forming inserts made on a consumer resin three-dimensional (3D) printer. OpenMFD generates matched photomasks and insert arrays from shared device dimensions. Matching pin and hole positions allow the entire insert array to be aligned and bonded to the patterned wafer in one operation. As an example application, we fabricated polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) plates containing 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on a standard 9 mm grid, eliminating individual well punching during device production. The plates supported multichannel liquid handling, automated imaging on two high-content microscopes, directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices and retrograde axonal labeling. OpenMFD also generated matching fabrication files for alternative compartment layouts.

## Introduction
Compartmentalized neuronal cultures enable independent access to cell bodies, axons and their local extracellular environments. Microchannel-connected culture chambers permit spatially restricted treatments, isolation of axonal material and analysis of axonal injury, regeneration and transport (Taylor et al., 2005). Subsequent designs have enabled controlled axon-guidance gradients (Taylor et al., 2015), localized neuron–glia interactions (Park et al., 2012) and modular neuronal culture models (Megarity et al., 2022). The geometry of connecting microchannels also influences device performance and the study of communication between neuronal populations (Goshi et al., 2022).

Parallel neuronal assays require space for multiple treatment conditions and biological replicates, together with access for liquid handling and imaging. Moll et al. (2024) addressed this need with a hard-plastic plate in a standard 384-well format containing up to 96 experimental units, typically comprising three open culture compartments connected by microchannels; functional recordings used two adjacent compartments as the experimental unit.

Other formats allow the culture layout itself to be changed. Megarity et al. (2022) assembled molded polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) modules into neuronal networks, demonstrating configurations of up to six modules. The modules can be assembled without specialist equipment and subsequently separated for reuse; larger assemblies used additional PDMS around the interfaces to improve sealing. Nebuloni et al. (2024) instead formed liquid boundaries around neuronal cultures under a fluorocarbon overlay, allowing the walls to be removed or rebuilt during experiments. Their jet-printing and dispensing system produced 21 circuits in a 6 cm Petri dish, each with two chambers joined by a conduit, and supported cultures for weeks, including axon-cutting and regeneration experiments. For laboratories making compartmentalized devices routinely, the fabrication and assembly steps also determine how readily such experiments can be repeated.

A persistent fabrication challenge in microfluidic cell culture is combining micrometer-scale channels with millimeter-scale, pipette-accessible reservoirs. Conventional soft lithography reproduces fine mold features in PDMS, a silicone rubber, but culture reservoirs are often opened individually after casting. For a 96-well plate, this would require 96 repeated hand alignments and cuts for every cast, slowing production and making the opening positions and shapes depend on manual placement. Channel-forming features near the 10 µm design width used in our device can be difficult to reproduce directly with consumer resin printers. SU-8 photolithography can form these fine features, while printed structures provide the larger well geometry (Venzac, 2025). Hybrid micro–macro molding has combined milled (machine-cut) and hot-embossed (heat-pressed) structures to replicate microscopic channels and macroscopic reservoirs without manual punching (Park et al., 2010), including devices for localized neuron–glia interactions (Park et al., 2012). Kajtez et al. (2020) deposited PDMS onto a patterned wafer, reproducing microchannels 8 µm wide and 4.7 µm high from a lithographic master while printing the larger compartment walls. This method also avoided manual cutting and well punching (fabrication comparison in Supplementary Data S6).

For academic laboratories, access to a shared cleanroom for reusable mold fabrication can be more practical than acquiring dedicated precision molding or machining equipment. Light-cured resin 3D printing offers accessible fabrication of larger structures and complex geometries, although printed resins can inhibit PDMS curing and may require additional treatments before use as casting molds (Venzac, 2025). Hybrid assemblies combining soft lithography with printed polymer components provide another route to customizable biological microfluidic systems (Meynard et al., 2025). Parylene C, a thin polymer coating, has also protected printed resins from erosion and prevented cytotoxicity toward primary cells (Musgrove et al., 2023). Ristola and colleagues combined fine features in SU-8, a light-sensitive resin, with individually positioned 3D-printed metal inserts to fabricate co-cultures of neurons and myelin-forming glial cells, or oligodendrocytes (Ristola et al., 2019).

We developed OpenMFD, an open-source set of programmatic tools for prototyping multi-well microfluidic plates from parametric presets and geometric building blocks. Changing design parameters, such as well size, compartment spacing or array size, requires coordinated changes across the photomasks and 3D-printed parts so their features remain aligned. OpenMFD generates the masks, insert arrays and plate-frame model from the same dimensions, allowing the whole insert array to be aligned and bonded to the patterned wafer in one operation (Figure 1). Photolithography defines the fine channel-forming features, while the retained printed inserts form the culture wells during each PDMS cast, removing individual well punching from repeated production. We demonstrate this workflow in a neuronal plate containing 48 two-compartment devices and 96 wells on a standard 9 mm grid, with a frame that fits standard microscope plate handling. The assembled plates supported multichannel pipetting and imaging on both Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal high-content microscopes without instrument modification. We also evaluated repeated casting, directional tracer movement, and primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices with retrograde axonal labeling. OpenMFD generates matching fabrication files for alternative compartment layouts. Once a mold is made, further plates can be cast and assembled on the bench without repeating cleanroom processing.

## Materials and Methods

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
To keep the mold and plate components matched, we specified the device geometry and plate layout once in OpenMFD (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`; Supplementary Protocol S2). The input was a Python preset: an editable set of parameters describing the well positions and sizes, channel geometry, device spacing and array size, together with mold dimensions such as insert height and the space between each pin and its hole (Figure 1A). The software repeated the device across the selected grid and generated the single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), well-forming inserts (Figure 1D) and plate frame (Figure 1E). The mask and insert files shared the positions of the well features, pins and alignment holes, allowing the entire insert array to be aligned with the patterned wafer rather than positioning each insert separately. By keeping the fabrication files coordinated, the parametric design let us rapidly refine the dimensions and gaps needed for reliable fit and sealing during prototyping.

OpenMFD converted these parameters into geometric models using the SolidPython library and OpenSCAD design software. Mask layers were exported as two-dimensional DXF drawings for photolithography; inserts and frames were exported as three-dimensional STL models for printing. Wafer outlines and cutting guides were generated from the same dimensions. Matching solid and hollow marks aligned the photolithographic layers, while matching pin and hole positions aligned the printed inserts with the well-and-hole layer patterned in SUEX dry-film photoresist.

The tested layout placed 48 two-compartment devices on the standard 9 mm well grid, using a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart (Supplementary Table S1). Each device had 62 microchannels with a design width of 10 µm and an interchannel gap of 30 µm. To compensate for PDMS shrinkage during curing at 100°C, matching masks and inserts were enlarged horizontally by 2.26% (scale factor 1.0226).

We also tested layout generation beyond the neuronal device by producing matched photomask drawings, insert models, wafer outlines and frame models for three literature-inspired layouts (Supplementary Table S1). These were an oligodendrocyte myelination layout (Kerman et al., 2015), an axon-guidance layout with gradients at right angles to each other (Taylor et al., 2015), and a three-compartment assay (Coquinco et al., 2014).

### Hybrid mold fabrication
We first patterned the wafer to form the channel features and well bases with insert-alignment holes. We then printed the resin inserts, aligned and epoxy-bonded them to the wafer, washed and cured the assembly, and coated the completed mold with parylene C.

On a 6-inch silicon wafer, we flood-exposed the first SU-8 2005 layer without a mask to form an adhesion base that helped the fine channel-forming features remain attached during later processing. A second SU-8 2005 layer was patterned through the microchannel mask; both layers had a 5 µm target thickness. Before SUEX lamination, we hard-baked the developed SU-8 stack to improve adhesion (Supplementary Note S2). The 200 µm SUEX K200 dry film defined the well bases and insert-alignment holes. We filtered its exposure with an LP360 filter to reduce raised edges at the insert-bonding surface (Supplementary Note S1).

Keeping the inserts on their detachable, flexible metal build plate preserved their relative positions, allowing the entire array to be aligned and bonded to the wafer in one operation. The inserts were printed from Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin on an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro, a consumer resin printer that uses an LCD mask to expose each layer (masked stereolithography, MSLA; Supplementary Data S2). The square pins were designed to leave 75 µm of space on each side inside their matching SUEX holes. EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy bonded the array to the wafer. The assembly was clamped for washing and curing (Figure 2C). A compressible EPDM rubber backing accommodated pin-height differences under clamping (Figure 2D). After washing and curing, the carrier was flexed away, leaving the bonded inserts on the wafer.

We coated the bonded mold with 1 µm parylene C to reduce potential cytotoxicity from the printed resin, protect the mold surface from material degradation and aid PDMS release (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Design dimensions refer to the mold before parylene coating.

### PDMS casting and plate assembly
We mixed Sylgard 184 PDMS at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio and poured approximately 30 mL per wafer mold, below the insert tops to leave the wells open. The wafer rack allowed multiple filled molds to be degassed and cured in parallel; curing was for 1 h at 100°C (Figure 3, steps 1–4; Supplementary Protocol S1). Tapered mold features aided demolding (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019).

We demolded the array and trimmed it along molded cutting guides to 108 × 72 mm. Plasma treatment activated the PDMS and glass surfaces before we bonded the array to a 110 × 74 × 0.17 mm glass sheet for optical access. We checked the PDMS–glass seal before autoclaving (Figure 3, steps 5–7).

For plate handling, the frame needed to remain flat through printing and warm, humid incubation, while its joint to the glass-bonded array needed to tolerate flexing without a heat-curing step that could warp the printed frame. We therefore selected high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a rubber-modified plastic, from the low-moisture-absorption filaments tested (Supplementary Data S3). LOCTITE SI 5140, a flexible room-temperature-curing silicone sealant, joined the array to the frame (Henkel, 2023). Because this assembly directly surrounded the culture wells, we screened candidate adhesives in complete framed devices for joint integrity and primary cortical-neuron survival; cell toxicity was not quantified. After the adhesive cured, a second dry-air plasma treatment made the exposed surfaces hydrophilic for aqueous coating and culture (Figure 3, steps 8–9).

### Mold and device characterization
Micrometer measurements of all 96 pins in one printed array guided the choice of EPDM backing (Supplementary Data S1). Reused molds and casts were checked for attached patterned features, bonded inserts and open wells and channels (Supplementary Protocol S2). Nine apparent channel widths were measured from brightfield images at three positions in one interior device per plate (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Data S4).

Plate-format compatibility was checked by filling eight wells in parallel with a multichannel pipette and collecting images on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal microscopes without custom handling or instrument modification.

### Pre-culture seal and tracer-movement check
Before neuronal culture, we used fluorescent tracers to check the assembled-device seal and observe movement between its connected compartments under an imposed volume difference. In each of 24 two-compartment devices, Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in PBS was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. We imaged the devices immediately after loading and after 72 h in a cell-culture incubator without replenishment or volume adjustment. Tracer movement was assessed visually by comparing green fluorescence appearing in the initially lower-volume compartment with red fluorescence appearing in the opposite compartment. Flow rate and the duration of the pressure difference were not measured.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
To test whether the assembled devices supported neuronal culture and axonal growth, primary cortical neurons were isolated from one embryonic day 18 (E18) Sprague Dawley rat embryo following Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates (Supplementary Protocol S1). Routine culture maintenance followed the cited protocol, with the device-specific conditions described below. The three plates were technical replicates of a single neuronal preparation.

To promote neuronal attachment, we coated the wells with dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences), selected based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water.

We seeded the cell-body (soma) compartment with 5,000–10,000 neurons in 50 µL medium per well. The seeding medium was Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After at least 30 min for attachment, we switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). To bias fluid movement toward the distal compartment, on the opposite side of the microchannels, we used 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment.

To test whether the cultured neurons extended axons into the distal compartment, we applied Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) to that compartment. The tracer was added at 1 µg/mL on day 10 in vitro (DIV10). Cultures were maintained to DIV11. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis was restricted to the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges. Five devices were excluded from the cell-count analysis because of segmentation or cell-count artifacts (Supplementary Data S5); cell-count value alone was not an exclusion criterion.

### Imaging and image analysis
Tracer and CTB images were acquired in widefield mode on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning and 100 ms fluorescence exposures. The tracer check used red and green channels suited to its fluorophores. CTB imaging used laser autofocus with 640 nm excitation and 706 nm emission; accompanying brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm.

CTB images were collected at 35 positions (7 × 5 grid) on Plates 1 and 2 and 42 positions (7 × 6 grid) on Plate 3, with approximately 10% overlap, then stitched into one image per device. To compare counts over the same device area despite this difference in coverage, each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to a common area. One cell count was recorded per analyzed device. These were baseline images collected before axotomy; Figure 4C reports the mean and standard deviation across analyzed interior devices for each plate (Supplementary Protocol S1).

## Results

### OpenMFD coordinates a hybrid mold that forms all 96 wells
Producing a whole plate from a reusable mold required the channel patterns and well-forming inserts to line up across the array. OpenMFD generated the single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), resin-insert models (Figure 1D) and plate frame (Figure 1E) from one set of device dimensions (Figure 1A). Matching pin and hole coordinates allowed all 96 printed inserts to be positioned against the patterned wafer together (Figure 2A–B). The completed mold combined fine SU-8 channel-forming features, a SUEX well layer and millimeter-tall resin inserts. Each PDMS cast then formed all 96 well openings without individual punching, and assembly produced the plate-format device shown in Figure 1F.

The completed photolithographic stack retained its fine SU-8 channel-forming features through later processing and provided a SUEX surface suitable for insert bonding (Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Note S2). Square SUEX holes defined the insert-pin positions (Figure 2F).

Pin heights in one measured array spanned 116 µm (Supplementary Data S1). The compressible EPDM rubber backing accommodated this variation so that all pins could seat in their matching SUEX holes together (Figure 2C–D), without separate adjustment of individual inserts.

After transfer, the inserts remained bonded to the wafer (Figure 2E). After parylene coating, the molds released cured PDMS without a silane release treatment (silanization), and microscopy showed open channels in the casts. Distances between the centres of opposing dark bands at the channel walls ranged from 10.7 to 13.1 µm across nine measurements in three cast devices (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Data S4).

Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained active for more than 8 months without visible degradation. Five molds were assembled with the final protocol (Supplementary Protocol S2).

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
The standard 9 mm well spacing allowed parallel pipetting without custom tip spacing: eight wells were filled together with a multichannel pipette. Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array, and multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. Repeated plate production therefore used the existing mold geometry rather than repeating insert alignment or individual well punching.

HIPS frames joined to the glass-bonded PDMS arrays with LOCTITE SI 5140 formed flexible joints that remained intact during repeated incubator transfers. HIPS frames showed less visible warping than polypropylene frames in the printing trials, consistent with the reported challenges of polypropylene extrusion printing (Spoerk et al., 2020). LOCTITE SI 5140 was the only screened adhesive that supported primary cortical-neuron cultures through the experimental endpoint.

Culture was poorer at the edges of the framed plates. In one unframed PDMS-on-glass array placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish, cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere. The outer PDMS positions could therefore support culture in that arrangement. Removing the frame also removed its adhesive seal and changed the culture vessel, so the comparison did not isolate the cause of poorer edge growth.

Both the Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal focused and collected images without custom plate handling or instrument modification. The dye and CTB images shown were collected on the Opera Phenix Plus, which was faster for these experiments.

### Directional tracer redistribution and neuronal culture
Green tracer redistributed toward the initially lower-volume compartment after one unequal-volume loading (Figure 4A). Images of 24 two-compartment devices acquired immediately after loading and at 72 h showed green fluorescence entering the left compartment, with little visible red signal in the opposite direction. This redistribution is consistent with the initial volume difference driving tracer movement; the two imaging time points do not establish how long that difference persisted.

Cortical neurons grew in the assembled plates and could be labeled from the distal compartment (Figure 4B). Primary cortical neurons from one E18 rat embryo were distributed across three assembled plates. CTB-647 was added to the distal compartment on DIV10, and labeled cell bodies were imaged on DIV11. This labeling is consistent with tracer uptake by axons extending into the distal well and transport back toward their cell bodies.

Cell-count analysis covered the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the edges, giving 72 devices across three plates. Five devices were excluded because of segmentation or cell-count artifacts, leaving 67 analyzed devices: 22 from Plate 1, 21 from Plate 2 and 24 from Plate 3. Figure 4C summarizes the CTB-positive cell-body counts for each plate.

### Shared dimensions support changes in device layout
OpenMFD generated matching masks and insert arrays for three additional compartment layouts (Supplementary Table S1). The myelination layout placed four wells around intersecting channel regions (Figure 5A–B), while the axon-guidance layout arranged four wells for gradients at right angles to each other (Figure 5C–D). The three-compartment layout connected its compartments in series (Figure 5E–F). Each pair shows the photomasks followed by the corresponding insert models. Changes in device spacing, well arrangement and channel design updated the mask and insert files together, keeping their designed positions matched. These additional layouts were generated as fabrication files but were not fabricated or biologically tested.

## Discussion
Reusable hybrid molds separate one-time insert alignment from repeated plate production. Three routinely used molds together produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation (Supplementary Protocol S2). Once the well-forming inserts are aligned and bonded, each cast forms all well openings from the same assembly. Further plates can therefore be made by benchtop casting and assembly without repeating cleanroom processing.

Combining photolithography with resin printing allows fine channel features and larger well shapes to be formed in the same mold. Resin-printing methods can produce casting molds or parts that remain in the finished culture device (Meynard et al., 2025). Treating the resin can improve PDMS curing and release, but reliably printing features near 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). In the hybrid mold, photolithography forms the fine features and the printed inserts form the wells. Parylene C coats the mold surface, and the inserts remain bonded for subsequent casts; the culture chambers are made of PDMS bonded to glass. The coating can be applied locally or by an external service.

Collective transfer extends insert alignment from individual pieces to a complete array. Ristola et al. (2019) combined fine SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts in a device for culturing neurons with myelin-forming glial cells, or oligodendrocytes. Shared pin and hole coordinates allowed all 96 resin inserts in the neuronal plate mold to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in one operation. The square pins constrain sideways movement in the patterned SUEX holes, while the EPDM rubber backing accommodates differences in pin height. These two functions let the inserts align and seat together. For layouts that fit on one wafer and print platform, the alignment operation remains the same as the insert number or arrangement changes.

The chamber is wider than the channel array, leaving a 1.275 mm margin on each side (Supplementary Table S1). This space allows lateral displacement of the well edge before it reaches the channel array; the margin is 17 times the designed 75 µm gap between each pin and its hole. Both distances come from the design; actual alignment also depends on printing accuracy and alignment of the photolithographic layers. The insert's adhesive-contact rim, or skirt, provides the region for bonding to the wafer.

Generating the masks and inserts from shared dimensions keeps their designed positions matched when a layout changes. The same changes update the wafer outline and frame. The neuronal layout was fabricated and used for culture; the three additional sets of photomasks and insert models in Figure 5 were generated as fabrication files only.

The size and shape of the wells link working volume to the plate dimensions. The neuronal plate accommodates up to 50 µL per well on a 9 mm well grid over a 110 × 74 mm glass sheet (Supplementary Table S1). Its sloped, or tapered, walls help the cured PDMS release from the mold but also affect well capacity. Increasing well capacity requires considering well height and taper together with well spacing and the supporting glass-sheet dimensions.

Shrinkage compensation and parylene coating change dimensions at different stages of fabrication. The masks and inserts receive the same 2.26% horizontal enlargement to compensate for PDMS shrinkage during curing. The mold dimensions specified in the design describe the features before coating. A uniform 1 µm parylene film would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge because both sides receive the film. Its height relative to the coated base would remain approximately unchanged because the ridge and base would both be coated (Supplementary Table S1).

Brightfield images provided apparent widths from the spacing of the visible channel-wall bands. The nine distances from three devices, measured between the centres of opposing dark bands, ranged from 10.7 to 13.1 µm (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Data S4). At the recorded calibration, moving each endpoint by one pixel can change a span by up to 2.37 µm. This change describes sensitivity to selecting the band centres; it does not bound physical measurement error. The images show lateral band spacing and do not determine channel depth or the full cross-sectional profile.

Tracer redistribution was consistent with movement toward the initially lower-volume compartment. Images at loading and 72 h documented the change after one unequal-volume loading, but did not establish how long the initial volume difference persisted.

The assembled plates supported routine handling, imaging and retrograde axon labeling in interior neuronal cultures. Compartmentalized neuronal cultures allow axons to be treated separately from their cell bodies. CTB applied to the distal compartment has been used to identify neurons whose axons reach it in studies of neuronal signaling and disease (Maimon et al., 2021; Moya-Alvarado et al., 2023).

Three plates received cortical neurons from one embryonic day 18 (E18) rat embryo, making them technical replicates of a single preparation. Analysis covered the 24 interior positions of each 48-device plate because culture was poorer at the edges. Neurons grew at outer PDMS positions in one unframed control, showing that those positions could support culture in that arrangement. The control also removed the adhesive seal and changed the culture vessel, so it did not isolate the cause of poorer edge growth. Frame geometry and sealing can be revised independently of the PDMS device and reusable mold.

## Conclusion
Combining photolithography with collectively aligned resin inserts moves well formation into the reusable mold. In the 48-device neuronal plate, each PDMS cast formed all 96 wells, removing individual well punching from repeated production. OpenMFD generates the matching photomasks and insert models needed to align and bond the array. The assembled plates supported multichannel pipetting, automated imaging and compartmentalized neuronal culture in interior devices. Subsequent plates can be cast and assembled on the bench without repeating cleanroom processing. The open-source software also generates coordinated fabrication files for alternative compartment layouts.
