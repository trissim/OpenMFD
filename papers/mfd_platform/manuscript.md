# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas, Yonatan Morocz, Jathav Puvirajan, David Juncker, and Alyson Fournier  
**Affiliations:** McGill University  
**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Plate-format devices made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber, can combine spatially controlled cell culture with multichannel liquid handling and automated imaging, but pipette-accessible wells are often punched individually after curing. We developed a design and fabrication method for reusable silicon-wafer molds that combine microfeatures patterned in SU-8/SUEX light-sensitive resins with 3D-printed resin inserts to form all wells during casting. OpenMFD generates the photomasks and insert array from shared device dimensions so that the printed pins match the alignment-hole positions on the wafer. The inserts are then transferred and bonded as one array. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The tested plate contained 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on the standard 9 mm grid, supporting multichannel pipetting and imaging on unmodified high-content systems. Assays demonstrated directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture in interior devices, and retrograde axon labeling. Once the mold is made, subsequent batches use benchtop casting and assembly, with all wells formed in each cast.

## Introduction
Microfluidic devices give cell biologists spatial control over cell bodies, axons, local reagent exposure, gradients, migration, differentiation, and co-culture interactions. Established designs support applications across neuroscience, stem cell biology, cancer research, immunology, and developmental biology (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and commercial devices are available from suppliers including Xona Microfluidics and ANANDA Devices. Plate-format devices extend these capabilities to experiments with many replicates using multichannel pipettes and automated imaging. For academic laboratories, custom designs and affordable local fabrication are important.

Experiments with many biological replicates or treatment groups benefit from devices arranged for standard pipetting and long-term culture. Multiwell plates provide this format. Many academic laboratories make custom devices from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber. They use light-based patterning (photolithography) to fabricate a reusable mold, then cast the devices, remove them from the mold (demolding), and bond them to glass. Commercial plates and hard-plastic methods based on hot embossing (pressing heated plastic against a mold), computer-controlled milling, or injection molding provide alternatives. For many academic biology groups, having a shared cleanroom fabricate a reusable mold is more accessible than acquiring precision molding or machining equipment. The laboratory can then cast and assemble devices on the bench. Supplementary Table S1 compares the equipment and production steps used by these fabrication approaches.

The fabrication challenge is the difference in scale between micrometer-sized channels and millimeter-deep wells. Patterning the light-sensitive resin SU-8 reliably forms the channels, whereas pipette-accessible openings are commonly punched into the PDMS after curing. Laser removal of material (ablation), direct printing, hot embossing, hard-plastic fabrication, and modular inserts combine these scales using different equipment and operations (Supplementary Table S1). Resin 3D printing can produce molds for PDMS casting as well as parts incorporated directly into the finished device. The choice of printer, resin, print orientation, and post-treatment affects printing accuracy and biological compatibility (Meynard et al., 2025; Venzac, 2025). Manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have also been bonded to SU-8 molds to form open compartments around photolithographic microtunnels in neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019).

Our approach combines shared design dimensions with collective insert transfer to make a reusable mold. OpenMFD defines the microfeatures, insert pins, and matching SUEX alignment holes in the same coordinate system and applies the same correction for PDMS shrinkage to the masks and inserts. The matching positions allow the printed inserts to be aligned with the wafer as one array. A compressible backing accommodates pin-height differences during bonding. The bonded inserts remain in the mold and form every well in subsequent PDMS casts, so each batch follows the usual casting and assembly sequence.

In the tested plate, each cast formed all 96 wells across 48 two-compartment devices. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The assembled devices supported multichannel pipetting, automated imaging, and cortical-neuron culture in interior devices. Tracer redistribution and CTB labeling assessed transport and neuronal compatibility, while three additional generated layouts examined how matching masks, inserts, and frames change together.

## Materials and Methods
We followed the two-compartment device from design through culture to evaluate the complete fabrication and assay workflow. Supplementary Protocol S1 gives the routine casting, assembly, and assay procedures; Supplementary Protocol S2 gives the one-time mold-fabrication procedure.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
The photomasks, insert array, and frame need matching device positions and dimensions. OpenMFD (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`) generated these fabrication files, wafer outlines, and cutting guides from shared well, chamber, channel, and array dimensions (Figure 1A–E). Device shapes were defined in OpenSCAD design software using the SolidPython library and exported as two-dimensional DXF mask drawings for each layer and three-dimensional STL models of the inserts and frames. Matching solid and hollow marks were used to align the photolithographic layers. The pin positions in the insert STL and the hole positions in the SUEX photomask came from the same coordinates, allowing the printed array to be aligned with the patterned wafer as a unit.

The tested layout placed 48 two-compartment devices on the standard 9 mm well grid, using a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart. Each device had 62 microchannels with a design width of 10 µm and an interchannel gap of 30 µm. Matching masks and inserts received a horizontal scale factor of 1.0226 (2.26% enlargement) for the 100°C PDMS cure. Supplementary Table S2 gives the nominal device, channel, pin, taper, and frame dimensions used to generate the fabrication files.

We examined design flexibility by generating matched photomasks, inserts, wafer outlines, and frames for three literature-inspired layouts: oligodendrocyte myelination (Kerman et al., 2015), axon guidance with gradients at right angles to each other (Taylor et al., 2015), and a three-compartment assay (Coquinco et al., 2014). These additional layouts were generated but not fabricated or biologically tested (Supplementary Table S2).

### Hybrid mold fabrication
The mold combined patterned SU-8/SUEX features with printed resin inserts to span the difference between micrometer-scale channels and millimeter-deep wells. It comprised two 5 µm layers of SU-8 2005 liquid photoresist (Kayaku Advanced Materials), a 200 µm layer of SUEX K200 dry-film photoresist (DJ MicroLaminates), and a printed resin-insert array on a 6-inch silicon wafer (UniversityWafer ID857). These photoresists are light-sensitive resins used to pattern the mold features. SU-8 was spread by spinning the wafer (spin-coating) using a Laurell WS-650-8B, SUEX was laminated using an SK 335R6, and photolithographic layers were aligned and exposed using an EVG620. Unless otherwise noted, materials were processed according to the manufacturers' instructions; the SU-8 2005 procedure targeted a 5 µm layer. Changes to the manufacturers' procedures are described below and in Supplementary Protocol S2. No dehydration bake or HMDS adhesion-promoter treatment was used. Facility-supplied propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) was the developer solvent used to remove unexposed material from both photoresists.

**Layer 1 (base):** To keep the microchannel features attached during later processing, we added an SU-8 2005 base layer beneath them. This layer was spin-coated to a 5 µm target thickness, flood-exposed without a mask, and left undeveloped; Supplementary Note S2 describes the adhesion observations.

**Layer 2 (microchannels):** A second 5 µm SU-8 2005 layer defined the fine channels separately from the printed wells. This layer was exposed through the microchannel photomask and developed. Before SUEX lamination, the wafer bearing the approximately 10 µm SU-8 stack was placed directly in an oven at 150°C for 10 min, then removed without controlled heating or cooling.

**Layer 3 (wells):** The inserts required a flat patterned bonding surface. SUEX K200 dry film (200 µm) defined the well features and insert-alignment holes, and LP360-filtered exposure reduced the raised edges observed during development (Supplementary Note S1). The SUEX was exposed at 2800 mJ/cm² through this long-pass optical filter to block shorter-wavelength ultraviolet light. After exposure, the oven was heated from room temperature to 50°C at 1°C/min, held at 50°C for at least 12 h overnight, then switched off and allowed to cool. The wafer was submerged in developer and mixed for 20 min; the developer was replaced at 15 min. For the hard bake, the oven was heated to 120°C at 3°C/min, held for 30 min, then switched off and allowed to cool.

Printing the inserts at their final relative positions allowed the whole array to be transferred together. We used an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro and Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin. The profile used 20 µm layers, 5.5 s normal exposure, 50 s bottom exposure, 7 bottom layers, edge smoothing (anti-aliasing) at grey level 2, and no shrinkage or tolerance compensation in the print-preparation software. Supplementary Data S2 provides the complete printer profile; washing and UV post-curing are described in Supplementary Protocol S2. Each insert was designed with a 1.85 × 1.85 mm alignment pin corresponding to a 2.0 × 2.0 mm SUEX hole, giving 150 µm nominal total clearance (75 µm per side).

The detachable flexible metal build plate kept the inserts together during transfer. EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy adhesive was applied around each pin, and the mold was positioned until the pins seated in their SUEX holes. An EPDM rubber sheet (0.03125 inch thick; Shore A hardness 60) between the fixed magnet and removable build plate provided a compressible backing to accommodate differences in pin height; a second flat plate and clamps distributed pressure across the assembly (Figure 2C–D). The clamped assembly was washed in two successive 1 min acetone baths, thoroughly blow-dried, and cured at room temperature for 48 h. After curing, the assembly was cleaned in an ultrasonic acetone bath for 10 min to remove residual material. The flexible metal build plate was flexed to release the bonded array (Supplementary Protocol S2).

To separate the printed resin and adhesive from cast PDMS and aid demolding, we coated the bonded hybrid mold with 1 µm parylene C (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Parylene was deposited using a Specialty Coating Systems SCS 200 coater at the McGill Nanotools Microfab facility. Channel dimensions are specified before parylene coating; coated-mold and cast dimensions were not measured.

### PDMS casting and plate assembly
A printed frame supported the PDMS-on-glass device in a plate-format carrier. Frames were generated with the other fabrication files and printed in high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a rubber-modified plastic, on an enclosed Creality K1C using a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.20 mm layers, a 220°C nozzle, and a 100°C build plate. Supplementary Data S3 provides the frame-printing settings, alongside the supplied Creality Print 6.0.4 G-code file.

Keeping PDMS below the insert tops left all wells open during casting. PDMS (Sylgard 184) was mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio and poured over the full wafer within an aluminum-foil reservoir to this level. Filled molds were supported in a six-shelf metal wafer rack, degassed until visible bubbles were removed (typically 10–30 min; approximately −15 inHg in our setup), and cured for 1 h at 100°C. Multiple molds could be degassed and cured in parallel (Figure 3, steps 1–4).

The large mold features had tapered sidewalls to aid demolding, following the same design principle used in earlier multiscale PDMS molds (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019). The wafer was supported on a flat surface, and peeling proceeded from the corners toward the center while the PDMS was pulled backward to reduce local stress on the wafer.

The molded rectangular guide defined the cast footprint for glass bonding and frame assembly. We aligned this guide to an industrial paper guillotine and trimmed each cast to 108 × 72 mm (Figure 3, steps 5–6). The trimmed PDMS and a 110 × 74 × 0.17 mm coverslip-glass sheet were activated with dry-air plasma for 1 min at 200–600 mTorr in a Harrick PDC-001, brought into contact, and heated at 100°C for at least 1 min under uniform pressure (Figure 3, step 7; Supplementary Protocol S1).

The bonded PDMS-on-glass assembly was autoclaved in a sterilization pouch at 121°C for 15 min before frame assembly. The frame was decontaminated with 70% ethanol and inverted so that its adhesive groove faced upward. Henkel LOCTITE SI 5140 (3 oz tube, IDH 135264), a one-part silicone sealant that cures at room temperature, was dispensed into the groove (typically 0.5–1 mL per frame) (Henkel, 2023). The bonded device was inverted with the glass facing upward and seated into the frame. After a 3-day room-temperature cure, the assembly was turned upright and excess adhesive was trimmed with a razor blade (Figure 3, steps 8–9).

A second dry-air plasma treatment was used to prepare the assembled device for aqueous coating and culture after the frame adhesive cured (Harrick PDC-001; high power, 30 W, 10 min, 400 mTorr). This surface-treatment step was distinct from the earlier 1-min PDMS-glass bonding treatment.

### Mold and device characterization
We assessed insert seating and casting by measuring pin heights and inspecting the molds and devices. Before bonding, each pin height was measured individually with a micrometer at all 96 positions of one 8 × 12 printed array. Supplementary Data S1 contains the individual measurements and their deviations from the array mean. Molds and casts were inspected to check that the SU-8/SUEX features remained attached, the inserts were seated and bonded, and the cast wells and microchannels were open and intact. Microscopy was used to inspect the channels after coating and casting. Supplementary Table S3 reports mold numbers and usable-cast yield separately and defines a usable cast.

We selected frame materials by visually inspecting printed frames and assembled joints, and screened adhesives by whether primary cortical-neuron cultures survived to the end of the experiment. Cell toxicity was not quantified.

To test whether cells could grow at the outer positions without the frame, one PDMS device was bonded to glass without a frame or frame adhesive and placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish. This control removed both the frame assembly and the sealing effect of the frame adhesive.

We assessed routine plate handling by fitting eight tips to a 12-channel pipette and filling eight wells in one plate column in parallel. Compatibility with plate-format imaging was tested by focusing and collecting images on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal microscopes at the Early Drug Discovery Unit (EDDU), The Neuro, McGill University, without custom plate handling or instrument modification.

### Directional tracer-movement assay
The tracer assay tested redistribution after one unequal-volume loading. We compared fluorescence immediately after loading and after 72 h in 24 two-compartment devices. Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 in PBS was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. The volume difference was established once, and the devices were kept in a cell-culture incubator for 72 h without replenishment or volume adjustment. Images were acquired in widefield mode using a 10× objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.30, 2 × 2 camera binning, a 100 ms exposure, and red and green channels suited to the two fluorophores. Tracer movement was assessed visually by comparing green fluorescence appearing in the initially lower-volume compartment with red fluorescence appearing in the opposite compartment. Flow rate and the duration of the pressure difference were not measured.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
To test neuronal culture in the assembled devices, primary cortical neurons were isolated from one embryonic day 18 (E18) Sprague Dawley rat embryo following Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. Routine culture maintenance followed the cited protocol, with the device-specific conditions described below. The three plates were technical replicates of a single neuronal preparation. Dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences) was selected as the cell-attachment coating based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water. Neurons were seeded into the cell-body (soma) compartment in 50 µL Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 5,000–10,000 cells per well, allowed to attach for at least 30 min, and then switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). Volumes of 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment created a small pressure difference that directed fluid from the soma compartment toward the distal compartment during culture.

For retrograde axon labeling, Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) was applied to the lower-volume distal compartment at 1 µg/mL on day 10 in vitro (DIV10). Cultures were maintained to DIV11. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis was restricted to the 24 interior devices on each plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges. Devices were excluded only when contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in the final image; high or low cell counts alone were not grounds for exclusion. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete procedure.

### Imaging and cell counting
Widefield images of CTB-labeled cell bodies supplied the endpoint counts. We acquired these images at DIV11 on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus at EDDU using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, and laser autofocus. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm. Brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure; Alexa 647 images used 640 nm excitation, 706 nm emission, and a 100 ms exposure.

Cell counts were compared over the same device area despite differences in image coverage. Images were collected at 35 positions (7 × 5 grid) on Plates 1 and 2 and 42 positions (7 × 6 grid) on Plate 3, with approximately 10% overlap. These images were stitched into one image per device. Each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to the same area for cell counting, regardless of the number of imaging positions. One cell count was recorded per analyzed device. These were baseline images collected before axotomy; Supplementary Protocol S1 describes the dataset. The supplementary files include the microscope's saved settings and image positions for Plate 1 in XML format.

## Results

### Hybrid molds form all 96 wells in a single cast
OpenMFD used one set of device dimensions (Figure 1A) to generate single-device and wafer-scale photomasks (Figure 1B–C), matching resin inserts (Figure 1D), and the plate frame (Figure 1E). Matching pins and alignment holes allowed all 96 inserts to be positioned together on the patterned wafer (Figure 2A–B). The completed mold combined SU-8 microchannels, a SUEX well layer, and millimeter-tall resin inserts. Each PDMS cast therefore formed all 96 wells at once, eliminating individual well punching. Figure 1F shows the assembled plate-format device.

The final fabrication process provided flat bonding surfaces and attached microchannel features. LP360-filtered SUEX exposure substantially reduced surface ridges, while the flood-exposed SU-8 base layer and pre-SUEX bake supported adhesion through subsequent processing (Supplementary Figures S1–S2).

Collective bonding required all inserts to seat against the wafer despite differences in their height. Measurements of 96 pins in one array gave a mean height of 5.551 mm, a standard deviation of 26.7 µm, and a 116 µm range (Supplementary Data S1). The compressible EPDM backing accommodated this variation while the inserts engaged their matching SUEX holes (Figure 2C–D), allowing the array to be transferred without adjusting each insert separately.

The completed molds had seated, bonded inserts and retained SU-8 features (Figure 2E–F). After parylene coating, they released cured PDMS without a silane release treatment (silanization), and microscopy showed open channels in the casts.

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
The standard 9 mm well spacing allowed parallel pipetting without custom tip spacing: eight wells were filled together with a multichannel pipette. Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array, and multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. Repeated plate production therefore used the existing mold geometry rather than repeating insert alignment or individual well punching.

HIPS provided frames with less visible warping than polypropylene in our printing trials, consistent with the reported challenges of polypropylene extrusion printing (Spoerk et al., 2020). With LOCTITE SI 5140, the assembled plates supported primary cortical-neuron cultures through the experiment. The adhesive formed a flexible, visibly leak-tight glass-to-HIPS joint that remained intact during repeated incubator transfers.

Culture was poorer at the plate edges in the assembled devices. To examine whether the outer PDMS positions themselves could support neurons, we placed one unframed PDMS-on-glass device in a culture dish. Cells at the outer positions appeared to grow as well as those elsewhere. This control showed that those positions could support culture in that arrangement, but did not separate the effects of the frame and its adhesive seal.

Both the Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress Micro Confocal focused and collected images without custom plate handling or instrument modification. The dye and CTB images shown were collected on the Opera Phenix Plus, which was faster for these experiments.

Unequal reservoir volumes provide a passive way to bias movement between compartments. Images of 24 two-compartment devices acquired immediately after loading and at 72 h showed green tracer redistribution toward the initially lower-volume left compartment, with little visible red signal in the opposite direction (Figure 4A). This redistribution is consistent with the initial volume difference driving tracer movement; the two imaging time points do not establish how long that difference persisted.

### Neuronal compatibility demonstrated by CTB retrograde axon tracing
CTB retrograde tracing tests whether neurons can take up a tracer through axons in the distal compartment and carry it back to their cell bodies. Primary cortical neurons isolated from one E18 rat embryo were distributed across three assembled plates and cultured to DIV11. CTB-647 added to the distal compartment on DIV10 labeled neuronal cell bodies in the soma compartment (Figure 4B), consistent with uptake by axons extending into the distal well and transport back toward their cell bodies.

Biological analysis used the 24 interior devices per plate because culture performance was poorer at the plate edges, giving 72 devices across three plates. Three devices were excluded because contamination or a technical failure prevented cell counting in their final images, leaving 69 analyzed devices. Figure 4C shows the mean CTB-positive cell-body count per device and the standard deviation within each plate.

The same design method generated matching masks and insert arrays for the myelination layout (Figure 5A–B), the axon-guidance layout (Figure 5C–D), and the three-compartment layout (Figure 5E–F). ## Discussion
The method brings shared-coordinate design and collective insert bonding together to form a reusable mold. Ristola et al. combined SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts for a neuron-oligodendrocyte co-culture device (Ristola et al., 2019). Here, matching pin and hole positions allow all 96 resin inserts to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in a single operation. The square pins seat in matching SUEX holes, which limit sideways movement. Their nominal clearance is 75 µm per side, compared with a 1.275 mm chamber margin on each side of the channel array (Supplementary Table S2). This margin is 17 times the per-side clearance; final alignment also depends on printing accuracy and photolithographic layer alignment. For an array that fits on one wafer and print platform, the same alignment operation is used regardless of the number or arrangement of inserts. The compressible EPDM rubber sheet backing accommodates pin-height differences during collective bonding. The resulting mold forms every well during casting.

Recent resin-printing methods use both printed molds and printed parts retained in the finished culture device (Meynard et al., 2025). Post-treatment can improve PDMS curing and release from resin molds, but reproducibly printing features near 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). Combining SU-8 photolithography for the channels with resin printing for the wells accommodates both scales. Here, the resin inserts remain in the parylene-coated mold, and each finished culture device consists of PDMS bonded to glass.

Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The final process combined flat bonding surfaces, attached SU-8 features, and evenly seated inserts in a reusable mold. Once the mold is complete, laboratories can cast and assemble further devices without repeating cleanroom processing. Parylene coating can be performed locally or by an external service.

Changing device dimensions updates the matching masks, inserts, wafer layout, and frame together. Figure 5 illustrates this coordinated file generation for compartment, gradient, and co-culture layouts. Several dimensions must work together: pin and hole sizes determine alignment clearance, the insert skirt (the rim around its base) provides space for adhesive, and the sidewall slope affects demolding and well capacity. The same 2.26% shrinkage correction is applied to the masks and inserts.

Shared dimensions also provide a place to account for fabrication effects when adapting a layout. A uniform 1 µm parylene coating would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge while leaving its height relative to the coated base approximately unchanged. Measurements of coated molds and casts could inform a coating correction alongside the existing PDMS shrinkage correction (Supplementary Table S2).

Improving culture at the plate edges would make more of the cast array available for biological experiments. Biological analysis was restricted to the 24 interior positions of each 48-device plate. The unframed control showed that outer PDMS positions could support neurons, but the cause of poorer edge growth in assembled plates remains unresolved. Frame geometry and sealing can be revised independently of the PDMS device and reusable mold.

The present plate accommodates up to 50 µL per well while retaining tapered walls for demolding, the 9 mm well grid, and a 110 × 74 mm glass sheet (Supplementary Table S2). Larger working volumes can be considered by revising the well geometry and glass footprint together; larger custom-cut glass would increase fabrication costs.

Repeated casting and plate-format assays demonstrate a practical route from one-time mold fabrication to routine device production. Experiments across independent neuronal preparations and time-resolved transport measurements would extend the biological and fluidic characterization.

## Conclusion
OpenMFD links the design of matching photomasks and printed inserts to collective array alignment and bonding. The resulting reusable mold forms the microfeatures and wells in each PDMS cast, removing individual well punching from repeated device production. The demonstrated plates combine multichannel pipetting and automated imaging with compartmentalized neuronal culture in interior devices. By separating specialized mold fabrication from repeated device production, this approach allows laboratories to make subsequent batches by benchtop casting and assembly. The open-source design tools and fabrication procedure provide a starting point for adapting this approach to other plate-format culture devices.
