### R03. Abstract

**V7 P4:**

> Plate-format devices made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber, can combine spatially controlled cell culture with multichannel liquid handling and automated imaging, but pipette-accessible wells are often punched individually after curing. We developed a design and fabrication method for reusable silicon-wafer molds that combine microfeatures patterned in SU-8/SUEX light-sensitive resins with 3D-printed resin inserts to form all wells during casting. OpenMFD generates the photomasks and insert array from shared device dimensions so that the printed pins match the alignment-hole positions on the wafer. The inserts are then transferred and bonded as one array. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained in use for more than 8 months without visible degradation. The tested plate contained 48 two-compartment devices with 96 wells on the standard 9 mm grid, supporting multichannel pipetting and imaging on unmodified high-content systems. Assays demonstrated directional tracer movement, primary cortical neuron culture, and retrograde axon labeling. Culture validation was limited to interior devices because growth was poorer at the plate edges. Once the mold is made, subsequent batches use benchtop casting and assembly, with all wells formed in each cast.

**V8 P8:**

> Compartmentalized microfluidic culture enables spatially controlled neuronal assays, but scaling devices for parallel experiments can require laborious reservoir punching and assembly. We developed an open-source hybrid fabrication method that integrates photolithographically patterned microchannels with three-dimensional (3D)-printed well-forming inserts on reusable silicon-wafer molds. A parametric design tool, OpenMFD, generates matched photomasks and resin-insert arrays, allowing all inserts to be aligned and bonded in a single operation. The molds produce polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) plates containing 48 two-compartment devices and 96 wells on a standard 9 mm grid without individual well punching. Three molds yielded more than 50 usable casts; the most recent remained in service for over eight months. Finished plates supported multichannel pipetting, automated high-content imaging, directional tracer redistribution, and primary cortical neuron culture. Retrograde cholera toxin subunit B labeling was quantified in 69 interior devices across three technical-replicate plates. Peripheral culture performance was poorer, and final microchannel dimensions remain unmeasured. This hybrid fabrication workflow provides an adaptable route to plate-format microfluidic assays while separating specialized mold fabrication from routine device casting.

### R04. Introduction: applications

**V7 P6:**

> Microfluidic devices give cell biologists spatial control over cell bodies, axons, local reagent exposure, gradients, migration, differentiation, and co-culture interactions. Established designs support applications across neuroscience, stem cell biology, cancer research, immunology, and developmental biology (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and commercial devices are available from suppliers including Xona Microfluidics and ANANDA Devices. Plate-format devices extend these capabilities to experiments with many replicates using multichannel pipettes and automated imaging. For academic laboratories, custom designs and affordable local fabrication are important.

**V8 P11:**

> Microfluidic compartmentalization permits independent access to neuronal cell bodies, axons, and their extracellular environments, enabling localized treatments and studies of axonal transport, injury, and regeneration (PMID: 16094385). Subsequent platforms have supported axon-guidance gradients, neuron–glia interactions, and configurable neuronal networks (PMIDs: 26000554; 22828584; 35466333). Microchannel geometry influences neuronal culture behavior and device performance (PMID: 36111641). These applications increasingly require multiple treatment conditions and experimental replicates, creating demand for devices that retain spatial control while accommodating routine pipetting and microscopy.

### R06. Introduction: access to fabrication

**V7 P7:**

> Experiments with many biological replicates or treatment groups benefit from devices arranged for standard pipetting and long-term culture. Multiwell plates provide this format. Many academic laboratories make custom devices from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone rubber. They use light-based patterning (photolithography) to fabricate a reusable mold, then cast the devices, remove them from the mold (demolding), and bond them to glass. Commercial plates and hard-plastic methods based on hot embossing (pressing heated plastic against a mold), computer-controlled milling, or injection molding provide alternatives. For many academic biology groups, having a shared cleanroom fabricate a reusable mold is more accessible than acquiring precision molding or machining equipment. The laboratory can then cast and assemble devices on the bench. Hard-plastic methods require specialized equipment during each production run and may still require photolithography or another high-resolution process to define the micrometer-scale channels (Supplementary Table S2).

**V8 P56:**

> Several fabrication adjustments were necessary for reliable assembly, including filtered SUEX exposure, an SU-8 base layer and prelamination bake, compressible clamping, and release from a flexible printing plate. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts, and recent molds remained in service for more than eight months. Once fabricated, these molds allowed subsequent devices to be made by routine PDMS casting and plate assembly without repeating cleanroom photolithography or punching individual wells. This separation could benefit laboratories using shared facilities. However, two of five molds were lost during handling or casting, and manufacturing time, costs, and yield relative to other methods were not quantified. The process therefore demonstrates practical reuse but not yet an optimized production economy.

### R07. Introduction: ways to combine small channels and large wells

**V7 P8:**

> The fabrication challenge is the difference in scale between micrometer-sized channels and millimeter-deep wells. Patterning the light-sensitive resin SU-8 reliably forms the channels, whereas pipette-accessible openings are commonly punched into the PDMS after curing. Laser removal of material (ablation), direct printing, hot embossing, hard-plastic fabrication, and modular inserts combine these scales using different equipment and added operations (Supplementary Table S2). Resin 3D printing can produce molds for PDMS casting as well as parts incorporated directly into the finished device. The choice of printer, resin, print orientation, and post-treatment affects printing accuracy and biological compatibility (Meynard et al., 2025; Venzac, 2025). Manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have also been bonded to SU-8 molds to form open compartments around photolithographic microtunnels in neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019).

**V8 P13:**

> A particular fabrication challenge is combining micrometer-scale microchannels with millimeter-scale, pipette-accessible wells. Conventional polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) soft lithography replicates fine photolithographic features, but reservoir openings often require individual punching after casting. Previously developed multiscale molds have combined milling and hot embossing to eliminate punching (PMID: 20049640), while three-dimensional (3D) printing has enabled complex compartmentalized neuronal devices (PMID: 32832365). Resin printing offers accessible fabrication of larger structures, although its resolution and interactions with PDMS curing require careful processing and surface treatment (PMID: 40190179). Hybrid lithography–printing workflows have likewise been developed for biological microfluidics (PMID: 40667579).

### R12. Methods: SU-8 and SUEX layers

**V7 P17:**

> Hybrid mold fabrication

**V7 P18:**

> The mold comprised two 5 µm layers of SU-8 2005 liquid photoresist (Kayaku Advanced Materials), a 200 µm layer of SUEX K200 dry-film photoresist (DJ MicroLaminates), and a printed resin-insert array on a 6-inch silicon wafer (UniversityWafer ID857). These photoresists are light-sensitive resins used to pattern the mold features. SU-8 was spread by spinning the wafer (spin-coating) using a Laurell WS-650-8B, SUEX was laminated using an SK 335R6, and photolithographic layers were aligned and exposed using an EVG620. Unless otherwise noted, materials were processed according to the manufacturers’ instructions; the SU-8 2005 procedure targeted a 5 µm layer. Changes to the manufacturers’ procedures are described below and in Supplementary Protocol S2. No dehydration bake or HMDS adhesion-promoter treatment was used. Facility-supplied propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) was the developer solvent used to remove unexposed material from both photoresists.

**V7 P19:**

> Layer 1 (base): SU-8 2005 was spin-coated to a 5 µm target thickness, flood-exposed without a mask, and left undeveloped beneath the patterned microchannel layer (Supplementary Note S2).

**V7 P20:**

> Layer 2 (microchannels): A second 5 µm SU-8 2005 layer was exposed through the microchannel photomask and developed. Before SUEX lamination, the wafer bearing the approximately 10 µm SU-8 stack was placed directly in an oven at 150°C for 10 min, then removed without controlled heating or cooling.

**V7 P21:**

> Layer 3 (wells): SUEX K200 dry film (200 µm) defined the well features and insert-alignment holes. The SUEX was exposed at 2800 mJ/cm² through an LP360 long-pass optical filter to block shorter-wavelength ultraviolet light. After exposure, the oven was heated from room temperature to 50°C at 1°C/min, held at 50°C for at least 12 h overnight, then switched off and allowed to cool. The wafer was submerged in developer and mixed for 20 min; the developer was replaced at 15 min. For the hard bake, the oven was heated to 120°C at 3°C/min, held for 30 min, then switched off and allowed to cool.

**V8 P19:**

> Photolithographic mold fabrication — Two nominal 5 µm layers of SU-8 2005 (Kayaku Advanced Materials) and one 200 µm layer of SUEX K200 dry film (DJ MicroLaminates) were patterned on a 6-inch silicon wafer (UniversityWafer ID857). SU-8 was spin-coated (Laurell WS-650-8B), SUEX laminated (SK 335R6), and layers aligned and exposed (EVG620). Propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) served as the developer. The first SU-8 layer was flood-exposed and left undeveloped beneath the second, microchannel-patterned layer. Before SUEX lamination, the SU-8 stack was baked at 150 °C for 10 min. SUEX defined large chamber features and insert-alignment holes and was exposed at 2800 mJ cm⁻² through an LP360 long-pass filter.

**V8 P20:**

> Post-exposure SUEX processing comprised heating at 1 °C min⁻¹ to 50 °C, holding at least 12 h, and cooling in the switched-off oven. Wafers were developed for 20 min with a developer change after 15 min, then baked at 120 °C for 30 min after ramping at 3 °C min⁻¹. ### R41. Discussion: contribution and prior inserts

**V7 P68:**

> The method combines shared device coordinates with alignment and bonding of the entire insert array to form a reusable mold. Ristola et al. combined SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts for a neuron-oligodendrocyte co-culture device (Ristola et al., 2019). Here, matching pin and hole positions allow all 96 resin inserts to be aligned with a 6-inch wafer in a single operation. The square pins seat in matching SUEX holes, which limit sideways movement. Their nominal clearance is 75 µm per side, compared with a 1.275 mm chamber margin on each side of the channel array (Supplementary Table S3). This margin is 17 times the per-side clearance; final alignment also depends on printing accuracy and photolithographic layer alignment. For an array that fits on one wafer and print platform, the same alignment operation is used regardless of the number or arrangement of inserts. The compressible EPDM rubber sheet backing accommodates pin-height differences during bonding of the entire array. The resulting mold forms every well during casting.

**V8 P54:**

> This study establishes a hybrid fabrication workflow that integrates photolithographically patterned microchannels with well-forming 3D-printed inserts on reusable wafer molds. Its principal contribution is the combination of coordinated parametric design, simultaneous alignment of an entire insert array, and repeated casting of complete plate-format devices. Neither multiscale molding nor elimination of well punching is new: earlier work combined micro- and millimeter-scale features by alternative molding and additive manufacturing (PMIDs: 20049640; 32832365), while printed metal inserts were individually aligned to SU-8 masters for neuron–oligodendrocyte devices (DOI: 10.1088/1361-6439/ab16a7). Here, common design coordinates link the photomasks, insert array, and supporting components, extending the insert-based approach to 96 positions aligned in one operation.

**V8 P55:**

> The hybrid workflow exploits the complementary strengths of photolithography for fine channels and resin printing for taller reservoir structures. Printable molds and hybrid processes offer greater geometric flexibility but may encounter resolution and PDMS-curing limitations (PMIDs: 40190179; 40667579). In our design, printed components remain part of the mold rather than contacting cultured cells in the final PDMS–glass device; parylene C provides a barrier at the casting interface (PMID: 37534979). The nominal pin–opening clearance of 75 µm per side is smaller than the 1.275 mm channel-to-chamber margin, providing a geometric allowance for positioning. Nevertheless, actual alignment accuracy has not been measured and may depend on printing and photolithographic registration.

### R45. Discussion: parylene and well geometry

**V7 P72:**

> Parylene coating also changes the mold geometry. A uniform 1 µm coating would add approximately 2 µm to the width of a channel-forming ridge, a substantial change for the nominal 10 µm channels. The ridge height relative to the surrounding surface would remain approximately unchanged because the coating raises both the ridge top and the flood-exposed base. This predicts wider molded channels, with final dimensions also affected by PDMS shrinkage. Measuring the coated molds and casts would allow a coating-thickness correction to be added to the design parameters. Supplementary Table S3 summarizes these relationships and the checks needed when adapting the design.

**V7 P74:**

> Well geometry and the selected glass size also constrain the plate design. The tapered sidewalls needed for demolding leave less space for wells than in a conventional 96-well plate. At the fixed 9 mm well spacing, taper and the wall thickness between adjacent wells constrain both well width and depth (Supplementary Table S3). The safe maximum working volume in the present plate was 50 µL per well. Slightly larger wells were possible, but would have required a larger glass sheet to accommodate the array. Custom-cut glass would have substantially increased fabrication costs. Applications requiring more than 50 µL per well would therefore need a revised design that balances well capacity, demolding, plate dimensions, and glass cost.

**V8 P58:**

> Device geometry imposes constraints when adapting this method to other applications. A 2.26% horizontal scaling factor was used for PDMS shrinkage, but the finished cast microchannels were not measured. Uniform deposition of the nominal 1 µm parylene layer could widen a channel-forming ridge by approximately 2 µm, a potentially substantial change for a 10 µm nominal feature; imaging coated molds and finished casts would permit calibration. Tapered walls assist demolding but also restrict well width and depth on a fixed 9 mm grid. The present plate had a safe maximum working volume of approximately 50 µL per well. Greater capacity would require balancing sidewall taper, interwell wall thickness, glass dimensions, and expense.

### R48. Conclusion

**V7 P76:**

> Conclusion

**V7 P77:**

> OpenMFD links the design of matching photomasks and printed inserts to alignment and bonding of the entire insert array. The resulting reusable mold forms the microfeatures and wells in each PDMS cast, removing individual well punching from repeated device production. The demonstrated devices combine compartmentalized neuronal culture with multichannel pipetting and automated imaging, although culture performance at the plate edges requires improvement. By separating specialized mold fabrication from repeated device production, this approach allows laboratories to make subsequent batches by benchtop casting and assembly. The open-source design tools and fabrication procedure provide a starting point for adapting this approach to other plate-format culture devices.

**V8 P61:**

> Conclusions

**V8 P62:**

> We developed an open-source hybrid fabrication method in which coordinated photomasks and 3D-printed inserts form reusable wafer molds for medium-throughput microfluidic culture. Simultaneous alignment of 96 well-forming inserts allows repeated PDMS casting of 48 compartmentalized devices per plate without individual reservoir punching. More than 50 usable casts were obtained from three molds, and assembled devices supported multichannel pipetting, high-content imaging, directional tracer redistribution, and primary neuronal retrograde labeling. Future evaluation of cast-channel dimensions, fluidic performance, peripheral culture conditions, and biological reproducibility will be needed to establish broader assay robustness. The approach provides a customizable route to plate-format microfluidic device production.

### What those comparisons justify saying

Lin and Compera separate making molds from making devices. Moon and Taylor
combine fabrication, culture and imaging methods. Broeren includes both a
fabrication figure and a detailed supplementary protocol. These are published
examples of the choices we made. The table gives the exact headings and locations.
