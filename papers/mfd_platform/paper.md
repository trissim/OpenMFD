# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas, Yonatan Morocz, Jathav Puvirajan, David Juncker, and Alyson Fournier  
**Affiliations:** McGill University  
**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Plate-format PDMS microfluidics can combine spatially controlled cell culture with multichannel liquid handling and automated imaging, but fabricating pipette-accessible wells often involves fragile serial post-cure punching. We developed a platform to fabricate a reusable silicon-wafer mold that combines SU-8/SUEX microfeatures with an aligned array of 3D-printed resin inserts to form every well during PDMS casting, eliminating that repeated fabrication step. OpenMFD generates matching photomasks, insert arrays, shrinkage compensation, wafer layouts, and plate frames from one set of device dimensions. The reusable molds produced more than 50 usable casts. The demonstrated format contained 48 two-compartment devices and 96 wells within the outer dimensions of a standard plate and supported multichannel pipetting, high-content imaging, and directional fluid movement for 72 h. After 11 days in culture, primary cortical neurons extended axons into the distal compartment, and CTB added to that compartment retrogradely labeled neuronal cell bodies. OpenMFD also generated matching fabrication files for additional compartment, gradient, and co-culture layouts. By concentrating cleanroom processing and specialized assembly in a reusable mold, the workflow allows subsequent devices to be produced by parallel benchtop PDMS casting and assembly.

## Introduction
Microfluidic devices give cell biologists spatial control over cell bodies, axons, local reagent exposure, gradients, migration, differentiation, and co-culture interactions. Established designs support applications across neuroscience, stem cell biology, cancer research, immunology, and developmental biology (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and commercial devices are available from suppliers including Xona Microfluidics and ANANDA Devices. Plate-format devices extend these capabilities to experiments with many replicates using multichannel pipettes and automated imaging. For academic laboratories, custom designs and affordable local fabrication are important.

Routine experiments often require many devices for biological replicates and treatment groups, together with standard pipetting and long-term culture. Multiwell plates are therefore a common format. Many academic laboratories make custom PDMS devices by using photolithography to fabricate a reusable mold, then casting, demolding, and bonding each device to glass. Concentrating the more specialized fabrication steps in the mold preserves this familiar production workflow for plate-format devices. Commercial plates and hard-plastic methods based on hot embossing, CNC milling, or injection molding provide alternatives. For many academic biology groups, an established shared cleanroom offers a more accessible route to a reusable photolithographic mold than acquiring precision molding or machining equipment. A cleanroom facility can fabricate the mold, after which the biology laboratory can produce devices on the bench without operating the cleanroom equipment itself. Hard-plastic methods require specialized equipment during each production run and may still require photolithography or another high-resolution process to define the micrometer-scale channels (Supplementary Table S2).

The fabrication challenge is the difference in scale between micrometer-sized channels and millimeter-deep wells. SU-8 photolithography reliably forms the channels, whereas pipette-accessible openings are commonly punched into the PDMS after curing. Laser ablation, direct printing, hot embossing, hard-plastic fabrication, and modular inserts combine these scales using different equipment and added operations (Supplementary Table S2). Resin 3D printing can produce molds for PDMS casting as well as parts incorporated directly into the finished device. The choice of printer, resin, print orientation, and post-treatment affects printing accuracy and biological compatibility (Meynard et al., 2025; Venzac, 2025). Manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have also been bonded to SU-8 molds to form open compartments around photolithographic microtunnels in neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019).

Our approach uses a mechanically aligned array of 3D-printed resin inserts to form every well during casting, while SU-8/SUEX defines the smaller features. The printed resin remains in the reusable mold and forms the wells in each PDMS cast. OpenMFD keeps the two scales aligned by generating the photomasks, resin inserts, shrinkage compensation, wafer layout, and plate frame from one set of dimensions. After the hybrid mold is fabricated, each batch follows the usual PDMS casting and assembly sequence.

We evaluated the method using a compartmentalized neuronal culture device, from file generation and mold fabrication through repeated casting, plate handling, imaging, directional fluid-flow testing, and long-term culture. We also generated mask, insert, and frame files for three literature-inspired layouts.

## Materials and Methods
### Experimental workflow and supplementary protocol
The reusable mold is fabricated once in a microfabrication facility; subsequent PDMS casting and device assembly are performed on the laboratory bench. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides step-by-step instructions for device casting, assembly, primary-neuron culture, CTB retrograde axon tracing, and endpoint imaging. Supplementary Protocol S2 describes hybrid mold fabrication.

The two-compartment design was fabricated, assembled, and evaluated through directional fluid-flow testing, neuronal culture, and imaging. For the additional layouts, OpenMFD generated corresponding photomask, insert, wafer, and frame outputs.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
OpenMFD is an open-source Python layout generator for plate-format microfluidics and hybrid molds (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`). The user enters the well spacing, chamber size, channel geometry, and array layout once. OpenMFD then generates (i) photomasks for SU-8/SUEX microfeatures, (ii) alignment holes that position the printed inserts on the mold, (iii) 3D-printable insert arrays with alignment pins, adhesive-contact skirts, and tapered walls, and (iv) wafer and frame outlines for alignment, trimming, and packaging. It also provides reusable shape and array tools, alignment marks for multiple fabrication layers, and shrinkage compensation.

The generator represents geometry as OpenSCAD models (via SolidPython) and supports export to:

- **OpenSCAD (`.scad`)** for inspection and as an intermediate format
- **DXF (`.dxf`)** for photomask printing (via OpenSCAD CLI conversion with DXF normalization using `ezdxf`)
- **STL (`.stl`)** for 3D printing and visualization (via OpenSCAD/viewscad tooling)

Each fabrication layer is exported separately, with matching solid and hollow alignment marks. Insert pins, skirts, and tapered walls are generated together with matching alignment holes in the photolithographic layers. OpenMFD applies the same PDMS shrinkage correction to the masks and inserts, avoiding separate manual scaling after export. Changing the array size, well spacing, gap between each pin and its hole, wall taper, or cure compensation regenerates every dependent file from the same configuration (Figure 1). The configuration used here contains a 6 × 8 array of two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart, for 48 devices and 96 wells on the standard 9 mm grid. Local testing can establish printing, bonding, demolding, and handling conditions for other plate dimensions and well spacings.

### Hybrid mold fabrication
Fine microfeatures were fabricated from three layers of negative photoresist: two thin SU-8 2005 layers (Kayaku Advanced Materials) and one thick SUEX K200 dry-film layer (DJ MicroLaminates). Unless otherwise noted, SU-8 2005 was processed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations for a 5 µm target thickness.

SUEX K200 was used for the 200 µm top layer because the dry film provides a uniform surface for the well features and insert-alignment holes without the long bakes, coating defects, and stress gradients associated with very thick spin-coated SU-8. SUEX forms the 200 µm-tall well layer and the flat bonding surface; the printed inserts form the millimeter-deep wells.

The 5 µm microchannels were patterned in SU-8 2005 by photolithography on silicon wafers. The 200 µm well features were patterned separately in SUEX K200. Alignment marks were used to position the printed inserts relative to the SUEX well layer.

**Layer 1 (base):** SU-8 2005, 5 µm. The resist is flood-exposed (no mask) and is not developed. This base layer improves adhesion of the patterned microchannel layer (Supplementary Note S2).

**Layer 2 (microchannels):** SU-8 2005, 5 µm. The resist is exposed through a photomask defining the microchannel layer, followed by development. The resulting two-layer SU-8 stack is hard-baked for 10 min at 150°C before SUEX lamination. The approximately 10 µm stack is placed directly in and removed from the heated oven without a controlled heating ramp or cooldown.

**Layer 3 (wells):** SUEX K200 (dry film, DJ MicroLaminates), 200 µm. This layer defines the well features and the holes used to align the printed inserts. Exposure is performed through an LP360 filter at a dose of 2800 mJ/cm². Post-exposure bake (PEB) is performed in an oven by ramping from room temperature to 50°C at 1°C/min, holding at 50°C for at least 12 h overnight, then shutting off the oven and allowing it to cool. Development is performed by submerging the wafer in developer and mixing for 20 min, with a developer exchange at 15 min. A hard bake is then performed by ramping from room temperature to 120°C at 3°C/min, holding for 30 min, and ramping down by powering off the oven.

We used 6-inch silicon wafers (UniversityWafer, ID857), SU-8 2005 from Kayaku Advanced Materials, and 6-inch SUEX K200 from DJ MicroLaminates. SU-8 was spin-coated using a Laurell WS-650-8B spin coater, SUEX was laminated using an SK 335R6 laminator, and the layers were aligned and exposed using an EVG620 mask aligner. No dehydration bake or HMDS treatment was used.

Propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) supplied by the facility was used to develop both SU-8 and SUEX.

Well inserts were printed using an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro resin printer and Siraya Tech Sculpt Clear resin. The manufacturer reports a heat-deflection temperature of 180°C for Sculpt Clear, meaning that deformation under a standard load begins well above the 100°C PDMS cure used here (Siraya Tech, n.d.). OpenMFD applied the same horizontal shrinkage correction to the insert STL and photomasks so that their pins and holes remained aligned after PDMS curing. The pins were 150 µm narrower than the matching holes, accommodating side-to-side variation in printing, and pin height was measured across the array before bonding (Supplementary Table S1). The print profile used 20 µm layers, 5.5 s normal exposure, 50 s bottom exposure, 7 bottom layers, anti-aliasing at grey level 2, and no slicer shrinkage or tolerance compensation. Supplementary Table S4 provides the remaining motion, timing, washing, and UV post-cure settings for this printer-resin pair.

The inserts were printed in an array at their final relative positions. Each insert had a protruding alignment pin that seated in a corresponding SUEX alignment hole. Insert height set the molded well depth.

The array was retained on a detachable magnetic build plate, which transferred all inserts to the mold together. Low-viscosity epoxy was applied around each pin, and the mold was positioned until every pin seated in its SUEX alignment hole. A second flat plate and clamps distributed pressure across the contact surface. The clamped assembly was washed in two successive 1 min acetone baths, then thoroughly blow-dried to clear acetone and displaced epoxy before a 48 h room-temperature cure. A 10 min acetone sonication after curing removed residual material. Supplementary Protocol S2 provides the complete bonding sequence.

The alignment system has 150 µm total clearance: 1.85 × 1.85 mm pins fit into 2.0 × 2.0 mm square holes, leaving 75 µm per side. This gap accommodates variation in both photolithography and resin printing while keeping the complete insert array aligned. Supplementary Table S1 reports the measured pin-height variation accommodated by the compliant clamping stack.

To distribute clamping pressure across the insert array, a 0.03125-inch, 60A-durometer EPDM rubber sheet was placed between the fixed magnet and the removable magnetic build plate carrying the inserts.

After bonding, the hybrid mold was coated with 1 µm parylene C to separate the printed resin and adhesive from the cast PDMS and aid release during demolding (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Parylene was deposited using a Specialty Coating Systems SCS 200 coater at the McGill Nanotools Microfab facility. The specified channel dimensions are the design dimensions before parylene coating.

### PDMS device casting, packaging, and preparation for culture
PDMS (Sylgard 184) was mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio, degassed until visible bubbles were removed (typically 10–30 min; approximately −15 inHg in our setup), and cured for 1 h at 100°C. Filled molds were supported in a six-shelf metal wafer rack during vacuum degassing and oven curing. The cure temperature was below the manufacturer-reported 180°C heat-deflection temperature of the printed resin and the reported thermal limits of EPO-TEK 301-2 (Epoxy Technology, 2021; Siraya Tech, n.d.). PDMS was poured over the full wafer within an aluminum-foil reservoir, with the fill level below the insert tops so that the wells remained open. Multiple molds could be degassed and cured in parallel. After demolding, the PDMS and glass bonding surfaces were activated by dry-air plasma for 1 min, brought into contact, and heated to stabilize the bond.

The large mold features had tapered sidewalls to aid demolding, following the same design principle used in earlier multiscale PDMS molds (Park et al., 2010; Ristola et al., 2019). The wafer was supported on a flat surface, and peeling proceeded from the corners toward the center while the PDMS was pulled backward to reduce local stress on the wafer.

A rectangular guide molded around the array was aligned to an industrial paper guillotine to trim every cast to the same outer dimensions.

The trimmed PDMS device was bonded to a 110 × 74 mm coverslip-glass sheet, autoclaved in a sterilization pouch using a dry 121°C cycle, and mounted in a 3D-printed plastic frame. Before assembly, the frame was decontaminated with 70% ethanol and inverted so that its adhesive groove faced upward. Henkel LOCTITE SI 5140 (3 oz tube, IDH 135264), a one-part alkoxy-cure room-temperature-vulcanizing silicone sealant, was dispensed into the groove (typically 0.5–1 mL per frame) (Henkel, 2023). The bonded device was inverted with the glass facing upward and seated into the frame. After a 3-day room-temperature cure, the assembly was turned upright and excess adhesive was trimmed with a razor blade.

The frame adhesive was selected through qualitative screening with assembled devices. Primary cortical-neuron cultures in devices assembled with LOCTITE SI 5140 were visually indistinguishable from adhesive-free controls, with no observable cytotoxicity. It was the only tested adhesive that also met the mechanical requirements: it formed a flexible, leak-tight bond between the glass and HIPS and remained intact through repeated transfers into and out of the incubator. Acetic-acid-cure and ketoxime-cure RTV silicones and cyanoacrylate adhesives caused visible toxicity. Norland Optical Adhesive 81 (NOA81), a UV-curable adhesive reported to support HEK293T and other cell lines (Li et al., 2016), also appeared compatible with HEK293T cells in our tests. However, primary cortical neurons exposed to the cured adhesive died within a few days despite extended UV curing. Other UV-curable adhesives tested also failed the primary-neuron screen. EPO-TEK 301-2 was suitable for bonding the inserts to the mold before parylene coating, but its cured joint was too rigid for the glass-to-HIPS frame bond and fractured after repeated incubator cycling.

To determine whether the different culture conditions at the plate perimeter arose from the outer positions of the PDMS array, one PDMS device was bonded to glass without a frame or frame adhesive and placed in a 14.5 cm-diameter polystyrene culture dish. This control removed both the frame assembly and the sealing effect of the frame adhesive.

The frame model was generated with the other fabrication files. Frames were printed in an enclosure on a Creality K1C using HIPS filament, a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.20 mm layers, a 220°C nozzle, a 100°C build plate, 15% grid infill, two wall loops, three bottom layers, five top layers, a 5 mm outer brim, no supports, and disabled part-cooling fans. Supplementary Table S6 summarizes the profile; the complete Creality Print 6.0.4 configuration is retained in the distributed G-code.

After the SI 5140 cured, assembled devices received a second dry-air plasma treatment in a Harrick PDC-001 system (high power; 30 W, 10 min, 400 mTorr) to decontaminate the surface and make it hydrophilic before coating and culture. This differed from the earlier 1-min treatment used to bond PDMS to glass. Dry ambient air was used in the plasma chamber.

### Directional fluid-flow testing and plate-format imaging
Directional fluid movement was visualized for 72 h in 24 devices of the tested two-compartment design. Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 was loaded at 25 µL into the left compartment, and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:1000 was loaded at 50 µL into the right compartment. The volume difference was established once; the devices were then left without replenishment or adjustment and imaged immediately and after 72 h. Images were acquired in widefield mode using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, a 100 ms exposure, and fluorophore-appropriate red and green channels. We assessed whether fluorescence moved from the higher-volume green compartment toward the lower-volume red compartment.

Plate-format imaging compatibility was evaluated on Opera Phenix Plus and ImageXpress high-content screening microscopes using assembled framed devices. Autofocus and acquisition were performed without custom plate handling or instrument modification.

Eight tips were fitted to a 12-channel pipette and aligned with one plate column, allowing eight wells to be filled in parallel.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and CTB retrograde axon tracing
Primary cortical neurons were isolated from one E18 Sprague Dawley embryo using the established cortical-neuron preparation described by Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. The three plates are technical plate replicates from one biological preparation. Dendritic polyglycerol amine (dPGA; DendroTEK Biosciences) was selected as the neuronal culture substrate based on Clément et al. (2022). Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water. Neurons were seeded into the soma compartment in 50 µL DMEM containing 10% FBS at 5,000–10,000 cells per well, allowed to attach for at least 30 min, and then switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). Volumes of 50 µL in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment created a small pressure difference that directed fluid from the soma compartment toward the distal compartment during culture.

Cultures were maintained to DIV11. At DIV10, Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) was applied to the lower-volume distal compartment at 1 µg/mL. CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted 24 h later, with one count per analyzed device. Analysis included the 24 interior devices on each plate because the frame did not maintain equivalent long-term culture conditions at the perimeter. Devices were excluded only when contamination or a technical failure made the endpoint image uninterpretable; count magnitude was not an exclusion criterion. Supplementary Protocol S1 provides the complete procedure.

Endpoint CTB images were acquired at DIV11 in widefield mode on a PerkinElmer Opera Phenix Plus at the Early Drug Discovery Unit (EDDU), The Neuro, McGill University, using a 10×/0.30 NA objective, 2 × 2 camera binning, and laser autofocus. The effective pixel size was 1.187 µm. Brightfield images used a 10 ms exposure; Alexa 647 images used 640 nm excitation, 706 nm emission, and a 100 ms exposure. For each device, 35 sites were acquired in a 7 × 5 grid with approximately 10% overlap and stitched. Each stitched image was aligned to a reference brightfield image and cropped to the same device area before CTB-positive somata were counted. A representative Plate 1 acquisition record is provided with the supplementary source files.

## Results
We assessed device production, plate handling, high-content imaging, directional fluid movement, and long-term primary-neuron culture.

### Hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin molds cast wells in place
The hybrid mold adds plate-format wells to a conventional SU-8/SUEX mold using one aligned resin insert array. The finished mold combines SU-8 microchannels, a SUEX well layer, and millimeter-tall resin inserts on a silicon wafer. During PDMS casting, the inserts form all 96 wells at once. This replaces serial punching and produces the well array as part of the standard casting cycle.

(D) Single and arrayed 3D-printable resin-insert models. (E) Plate-frame model. (F) Assembled plate-format device.](figures/rendered/openmfd_design.pdf)

Process development identified uneven SUEX surfaces that prevented epoxy sealing, SU-8 microchannel delamination, and incomplete seating or detachment of the insert array. Exposing SUEX without an LP360 long-pass filter produced ridge-like edge artifacts, whereas the filtered exposure substantially reduced the ridges and provided a flatter insert-bonding surface (Supplementary Note S1). Patterning the thin microchannel layer directly on bare silicon led to delamination; adding a flood-exposed SU-8 base layer prevented the observed failure without a dehydration bake or HMDS treatment. A pre-SUEX hard bake provided a second adhesion control. Eight wafers were processed in parallel during one development batch, and omission of this bake allowed the SU-8 stack to warp and delaminate around the well features during cooling after SUEX processing, although some wafers remained intact. In a subsequent two-wafer batch that included the 150°C, 10 min hard bake, both wafers remained intact (Supplementary Note S2).

Variation in pin height introduced a separate assembly challenge. Pin height varied by as much as 116 µm across one 8 × 12 array (Supplementary Table S1), so rigid clamping did not apply uniform pressure at every position. A slightly compressible EPDM layer accommodated this variation and allowed all pins to seat without individually leveling them. Slow 1–3°C/min SUEX thermal ramps, transfer from a flexible magnetic build plate, and room-temperature epoxy curing were adopted to limit wafer stress and insert detachment during assembly.

The optimized process produced flat bonding surfaces, firmly attached SU-8 features, fully seated inserts, and parylene-coated molds that released cured PDMS without silanization. Microscopy of the resulting casts confirmed that the channels remained open and retained the intended neurite-scale dimensions.

Using the finalized fabrication protocol, five wafer-scale hybrid molds proceeded through insert bonding and final assembly. Three entered routine use and together produced more than 50 usable casts. One of the other wafers broke during demolding, and leaked PDMS cured beneath the other in the casting rack. Supplementary Table S5 defines a usable cast and reports the mold and cast outcomes. The most recent molds remained in active use for more than 8 months without visible degradation.

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
Each PDMS cast contained the complete well array. Multiple molds could be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel. The wells followed standard microtiter plate spacing, and the assembled devices were compatible with multichannel pipettes and plate-format imaging systems.

High-impact polystyrene (HIPS; rubber-modified polystyrene) and polypropylene were evaluated as frame materials with low moisture uptake (Hamrol et al., 2023; Malinowska et al., 2024). HIPS warped less in our printing trials and was selected for the assembled devices. This observation agrees with reports that polypropylene can deform during extrusion printing as it cools and crystallizes (Spoerk et al., 2020).

In the unframed PDMS-on-glass control cultured in a 14.5 cm polystyrene dish without frame adhesive, cells at the outer device positions showed visually comparable growth to cells elsewhere in the device. This result shows that the outer positions support culture independently of the frame assembly. The frame and its adhesive seal remain separate components for further optimization.

## Discussion
The aligned insert array adds millimeter-scale wells to an SU-8/SUEX mold while preserving the usual PDMS casting, demolding, and glass-bonding sequence. The specialized fabrication steps are performed once when making the reusable mold, which then forms the complete well array in every cast.

Ristola et al. combined SU-8 features with three individually aligned, 3D-printed steel inserts to make a compartmentalized neuron-oligodendrocyte co-culture device (Ristola et al., 2019). OpenMFD extends this hybrid-mold strategy to a full 6-inch wafer by transferring all 96 resin inserts as a single array and generating the photomasks, inserts, shrinkage compensation, wafer layout, and plate frame from the same device dimensions. The resulting mold forms all 96 wells in one casting cycle and produces devices compatible with multichannel handling and automated plate imaging. More broadly, OpenMFD provides infrastructure for researchers to translate purpose-built compartment, gradient, and co-culture assays into matched fabrication files and plate-format devices for their own biological studies.

Recent resin 3D-printing workflows combine printed molds with printed parts retained directly in the finished device to build modular, plate-compatible culture systems (Meynard et al., 2025). Post-treatments can prevent resin molds from interfering with PDMS curing and can help release the cured PDMS (Venzac, 2025). Resin-printing resolution continues to improve, but reproducibly forming features on the order of 10 µm across a plate-scale mold remains difficult with accessible printers (Venzac, 2025). SU-8 photolithography therefore remains useful for the smallest channels, while resin printing is well suited to the millimeter-scale wells. In our workflow, the printed resin insert array remains in the parylene-coated reusable mold, while each finished culture device is PDMS bonded to glass. Routine device production then requires a single PDMS casting cycle.

### Reusable mold and design flexibility
OpenMFD separates editable device dimensions from the fabrication sequence by generating matching photomask, insert, wafer, and frame files. The neuronal device was fabricated and evaluated in culture. Figure 5 shows matching fabrication files for three additional layouts that can be fabricated using the same workflow.

Generating these files together ensures the plate dimensions, photolithographic layers, insert array, alignment holes, shrinkage correction, and frame all fit and align with one another. Deriving them from the same dimensions reduces manual CAD editing and speeds design iteration. OpenMFD can therefore generate plate-format versions of established compartment, gradient, and co-culture designs (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014). The examples show that one design change can be applied consistently across all fabrication files and provide the starting point for fabrication and assay-specific validation.

Repeated casting depended on flat SUEX bonding surfaces, stable SU-8 adhesion, controlled thermal stress, and uniform insert seating. The LP360-filtered exposure, SU-8 base layer and hard bake, compliant clamping stack, and flexible transfer fixture established these conditions as part of the fabrication process and resolved the corresponding failure modes observed during development. Routine inspection remains useful for identifying handling damage.

SU-8/SUEX patterning, insert bonding, and parylene coating are performed once for each mold design. The three molds that entered routine use produced more than 50 usable casts, and the most recent remained active for more than 8 months without visible degradation. Parylene deposition can be performed locally or by an external coating service. Once the reusable mold is complete, recurring device production requires no further cleanroom processing and consists of benchtop PDMS mixing and vacuum degassing, oven curing, guillotine trimming, and plasma bonding. Multiple molds can be processed in parallel.

Performance depends on accurate insert alignment and sealing, adequate spacing between channels and the insert-mold boundary, and a stable plate frame. The HIPS frame produced different culture conditions at the plate perimeter, so the biological analysis used the interior devices. The unframed control supported growth at the outer positions, focusing further optimization on the combined carrier and adhesive seal. Because the frame is a separate component, a more rigid replacement and a revised seal can be evaluated without changing the PDMS device or reusable mold.

### Design adaptation and validation
The spacing between wells, outer plate dimensions, photolithography layers, insert pins and holes, tapered walls, shrinkage compensation, and frame outline remain aligned because OpenMFD regenerates these related features together when parameters change. The tested design used two-well units spaced 18 × 9 mm apart, wells on a 9 mm grid, and a 200 µm SUEX layer. Local validation can establish the operating range for new pin clearances, adhesive contact areas, channel-to-insert spacing, SUEX thicknesses, and outer plate dimensions. Supplementary Table S3 provides the demonstrated dimensions and corresponding validation checks.

## Conclusion
OpenMFD turns the formation of plate-format wells from a fragile serial operation into a parallel, repeatable part of PDMS casting. The hybrid mold combines a 5 µm SU-8 microchannel layer, a 200 µm SUEX well layer, and aligned millimeter-scale resin inserts to cast 48 compartmentalized devices and 96 pipette-accessible wells at once. Three molds produced more than 50 usable casts. The finished plates supported multichannel pipetting, automated high-content imaging, directional fluid movement for 72 h, and primary cortical-neuron culture with retrograde CTB tracing. Because OpenMFD generates the photomasks, inserts, wafer layout, and frame from the same device dimensions, the workflow can be adapted to other compartment, gradient, and co-culture designs. OpenMFD provides an open route from editable device design to a reusable mold and benchtop production of custom plate-format microfluidic devices.
