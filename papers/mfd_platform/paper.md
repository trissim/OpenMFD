# Plate-format PDMS microfluidic culture devices from hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin wafer molds

**Authors:** Tristan Simas, Yonatan Morocz, Jathav Puvirajan, David Juncker, and Alyson Fournier  
**Affiliations:** McGill University  
**Corresponding author:** Tristan Simas

## Abstract
Many useful PDMS microfluidic culture assays remain challenging at the scale expected for modern cell biology because they are small-format devices, require manual well punching, and are not directly aligned with standard multichannel pipetting or plate-imaging workflows. Here we present a plate-format PDMS microfluidic workflow that casts pipette-accessible wells directly into each device, eliminating post-cure punching while preserving SU-8-defined microchannels and barriers. The reusable mold combines high-resolution SU-8/SUEX microfeatures with aligned 3D-printed resin inserts that form the tall wells during PDMS curing. OpenMFD, an open-source design tool, makes the workflow practical by converting one set of device dimensions into matched photolithography, 3D-printing, and packaging files. Mold fabrication requires one-time facility access, after which device replication proceeds by benchtop PDMS casting and assembly. The demonstrated plate contains 48 two-compartment devices and 96 pipette-accessible wells on a 9 mm well grid and was compatible with multichannel pipetting and plate-based high-content imaging in the tested workflows. A minimal functional experiment using one primary cortical-neuron preparation distributed across three plates showed distal cholera toxin B (CTB) uptake and retrograde labeling in eligible interior devices. The filament-printed frame did not maintain equivalent perimeter conditions during long-term culture, so outer positions were excluded from the biological endpoint. An injection-molded carrier could address this packaging limitation but was not evaluated here. We also show that three additional published geometries can be represented as OpenMFD-compatible fabrication file sets, while noting that those layouts were not fabricated or biologically validated in this study.

## Introduction
Microfluidic devices let cell biologists ask questions that are difficult to address in standard multi-well plates: separating cell bodies from axons, exposing one compartment to a reagent while protecting another, maintaining local gradients, or studying migration, differentiation, co-culture interactions, and responses to spatially controlled perturbations. Decades of work have produced literature-validated device architectures for applications across neuroscience, stem cell biology, cancer research, immunology, and developmental biology (Taylor et al., 2005; Taylor et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018; Coquinco et al., 2014), and several low-throughput designs are now commercially available (Xona Microfluidics, Ananda Devices). However, the formats most accessible to academic labs still provide only a small number of independent culture chambers per device, require manual liquid handling, and are not directly compatible with multichannel pipettes or plate-format imaging systems. High-throughput plate-format versions exist, but they are often too costly or too fixed in design for routine academic prototyping.

Although many useful device geometries have already been described, translating them into a format that behaves like a standard multi-well plate remains difficult in practice. For routine biological experiments, the barrier is not only making one custom device; it is making enough identical devices to seed replicates, pipette them with standard tools, keep cultures alive, and image the result on instruments already used for plate assays. Standard academic soft-lithography workflows can produce custom PDMS devices, but scaling those designs to 96-well format has typically required either (i) commercial platforms that offer limited design flexibility or (ii) hard-plastic microfabrication methods such as hot embossing, CNC milling, or injection molding that rely on specialized equipment not commonly available in university cleanrooms. A key access distinction is whether specialized equipment is required for each device-production cycle or mainly once to create a reusable mold; many plate-format alternatives keep per-device fabrication tied to dedicated tooling, whereas academic PDMS workflows become much more accessible when replication after the initial mold can be performed on the benchtop. This fabrication gap limits the ability of academic groups to perform replicate-rich studies, dose-response screens, and automated imaging workflows with custom microfluidic devices. Dependence on commercial platforms also limits the ability to modify existing designs or develop assay geometries tailored to specific biological questions.

The core fabrication challenge is multiscale: cellular microfluidic assays often require microchannels and barriers on the order of a few to tens of micrometers, while robust, pipette-accessible wells demand millimeter-scale depth and volume. SU-8 photolithography reliably yields micron-scale structures, but creating millimeter-tall, large-area wells in SU-8 is difficult and often replaced by manual punching of cured PDMS. Other approaches can remove parts of this bottleneck, but usually at a cost: laser ablation and direct printing reduce process complexity while sacrificing fine-feature control; hot embossing and hard-plastic fabrication improve scalability but require specialized tooling; and modular insert approaches preserve SU-8 microfeatures but can be limited by manual placement and alignment burden (Supplementary Table S2). For example, SU-8 molds with manually placed 3D-printed metal inserts have been used to combine photolithographic microtunnels with open macro-compartments in PDMS neural co-culture devices (Ristola et al., 2019), but that strategy was demonstrated at device scale rather than as a plate-format, arrayed mold workflow that casts pipette-accessible wells directly across a standard multiwell footprint. A practical plate-format workflow therefore needs to preserve SU-8-defined microfeatures, remove post-cure punching, and keep the resulting device compatible with standard biological handling and imaging equipment.

In this work, we address the bottleneck by making PDMS microfluidic devices that can be handled more like a multi-well plate: the wells are already present after casting, the device fits standard plate hardware, and the microchannels remain SU-8-defined. To achieve this, we put the millimeter-scale wells into the reusable mold rather than adding them to each PDMS device after curing. 3D-printed resin well inserts are mechanically registered to SU-8/SUEX lock features and adhesively bonded to the wafer, producing a hybrid mold that casts plate-format wells in place while preserving fine SU-8-defined microfeatures. OpenMFD keeps the design coordinated by converting one set of user-specified dimensions into matched photolithography, insert, shrinkage-compensation, wafer, and package files. The workflow proceeds from design to use in three stages. First, the software converts the desired plate-format geometry into the matched files needed for fabrication. Second, SU-8/SUEX photolithography and bonded 3D-printed resin inserts produce a reusable hybrid mold. Third, PDMS devices cast from that mold are packaged and tested for multichannel pipetting, imaging, directional fluidic bias, and neuronal culture.

We demonstrate the full workflow with a compartmentalized neuronal culture device, spanning file generation, hybrid mold fabrication, repeated PDMS casting, plate-format handling, imaging, directional fluidic-bias testing, and long-term culture. We also use three additional literature-derived layouts to show that published designs can be expressed as OpenMFD-compatible mask, insert, and frame files, while treating those layouts as unfabricated design-generation examples.

## Materials and Methods
### Experimental workflow and supplementary protocol
The platform separates repeated PDMS device replication, the step biology labs perform routinely, from the facility-based step used to make the reusable mold. The validated workflow is described here at the level needed to reproduce the demonstrated compartmentalized-neuron device. Step-by-step bench instructions for post-mold device casting, assembly, primary-neuron culture, distal CTB exposure, and endpoint imaging are provided in Supplementary Protocol S1; one-time hybrid mold fabrication is expanded in Supplementary Protocol S2.

For each design, OpenMFD generates coordinated fabrication blueprints for photolithography, resin insert printing, and the final plate package. The same sequence is used across compatible designs: SU-8/SUEX mold fabrication, resin insert bonding, parylene coating, PDMS casting, device packaging, plasma cleaning, culture preparation, directional fluidic-bias testing, and imaging.

### OpenMFD design and fabrication-file generation
OpenMFD is intended to reduce the manual CAD coordination that would otherwise be required to convert a biological device layout into a mold, insert array, and plate package.

The workflow begins in OpenMFD, an open-source Python layout generator for plate-format microfluidics and hybrid molds (`https://github.com/trissim/OpenMFD`). A user specifies device dimensions such as well spacing, chamber size, channel geometry, and array layout once, and the software derives the matched physical outputs needed for fabrication. These include (i) photolithography masks for SU-8/SUEX microfeatures, (ii) top-layer lock features for insert registration, (iii) 3D-printable well insert arrays with pins, skirts, and tapered walls, and (iv) wafer and frame-related outlines used for alignment, trimming, and packaging. The generator also provides parameterized geometric primitives, array/tiling utilities, wafer-mask generation, multi-layer alignment marks, and shrinkage compensation.

The generator represents geometry as OpenSCAD models (via SolidPython) and supports export to:

- **OpenSCAD (`.scad`)** for inspection and as an intermediate format
- **DXF (`.dxf`)** for photomask printing (via OpenSCAD CLI conversion with DXF normalization using `ezdxf`)
- **STL (`.stl`)** for 3D printing and visualization (via OpenSCAD/viewscad tooling)

Layer separation is handled by exporting separate geometries for each fabrication layer (e.g., channel layer vs well/chamber layer), with corresponding **full vs hollow alignment marks** to support multi-layer registration. Features related to insert alignment are represented both as 3D insert geometry (pins, skirts, tapers) and as matching “lock” features (e.g., square holes) in the photolithographic layer(s). The same PDMS shrinkage-compensation factor is applied to the relevant mask and insert outputs, so thermal-cure compensation is handled as part of layout generation rather than by manually scaling files after export. The numerical values used for the demonstrated device are editable preset parameters rather than fixed platform constants; changing array size, well spacing, pin/hole clearance, taper geometry, or cure-temperature compensation regenerates the dependent masks, insert models, and package outlines from the same configuration (Figure 1). The demonstrated preset uses a 6 × 8 array of two-well device units at an 18 × 9 mm unit pitch, producing 48 devices and 96 pipette-accessible wells whose centers follow the standard 9 mm well grid. These values are not hard process limits; changing the footprint, unit pitch, or well spacing is a software-level parameter change, although new physical formats should still be validated locally for printing, bonding, demolding, and handling.

### Hybrid mold fabrication
Once the coordinate system is fixed in software, the first physical requirement is a wafer surface that can support both fine microchannels and a flat, mechanically registered bonding interface for the 3D-printed resin inserts.

We fabricate fine microfeatures using a three-layer negative photoresist stack consisting of two thin SU-8 2005 layers (MicroChem) and a thick SUEX K200 dry-film layer (DJ MicroLaminates). Unless otherwise noted, processing steps for SU-8 2005 follow the manufacturer’s recommendations for a 5 µm target thickness.

SUEX K200 was used for the 200 µm top layer because the dry film provides a uniform intermediate-height layer for chambers and insert-lock features without requiring very thick spin-coated SU-8. This avoids practical thick-resist processing problems such as long solvent-removal soft bakes, comet-like coating defects, edge-bead or flow artifacts, and bake-induced stress gradients. In this workflow, SUEX does not form the millimeter-scale wells; it provides the flat, registered SU-8/SUEX features needed to align and seal the 3D-printed resin inserts that cast the wells.

Microchannels and thin well-rim features are patterned in SU-8 using standard photolithography on silicon wafers. Alignment marks are included for later lock-and-key registration with the 3D-printed resin inserts.

**Layer 1 (base):** SU-8 2005, 5 µm. The resist is flood-exposed (no mask) and is not developed. This layer serves as an adhesion promoter: without it, thin SU-8 microchannel features patterned directly on bare silicon can delaminate from the wafer surface during development or subsequent processing (Supplementary Note S2).

**Layer 2 (microchannels):** SU-8 2005, 5 µm. The resist is exposed through a photomask defining the microchannel layer, followed by development.

**Layer 3 (tall SU-8/SUEX features):** SUEX K200 (dry film, DJ MicroLaminates), 200 µm. Exposure is performed through an LP360 filter at a dose of 2800 mJ/cm². Post-exposure bake (PEB) is performed in an oven by ramping from room temperature to 50°C at 1°C/min, holding at 50°C, then ramping down by powering off the oven. Development is performed by submerging the wafer in developer and mixing for 20 min, with a developer exchange at 15 min. A hard bake is then performed by ramping from room temperature to 180°C at 3°C/min, holding for 30 min, and ramping down by powering off the oven.

We use 6" silicon wafers (UniversityWafer, ID857). SU-8 2005 is sourced from Kayaku Advanced Materials, and SUEX K200 (6") is sourced from DJ MicroLaminates. SU-8 layers are spin-coated using a Laurell WS-650-8B spin coater, and SUEX K200 is laminated using an SK 335R6 laminator. Photomask exposure/alignment is performed using an EVG620 mask aligner. No dehydration bake or HMDS treatment is used; the flood-exposed SU-8 base layer provides sufficient adhesion for the microchannel layer without additional surface preparation.

For development of both SU-8 and SUEX layers we use propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) supplied by the facility.

Use of the LP360 long-pass filter during SUEX exposure was important for achieving a flat top surface on the SUEX features. Without the LP360 filter, we observed ridge-like edge artifacts that prevented a uniform epoxy seal when bonding the 3D-printed resin inserts to the wafer mold, compromising the insert-wafer interface (Supplementary Note S1).

During process development, the photolithographic stack exposed three failure modes that needed control: (i) delamination of 5 µm SU-8 microchannel features from bare silicon, (ii) ridge-like edge artifacts that produced a non-flat SUEX top surface and prevented uniform sealing to the 3D-printed resin inserts, and (iii) stress-induced lifting of the thick SUEX layer during thermal processing. These issues were mitigated by combining a flood-exposed SU-8 base layer beneath the microchannels, LP360-filtered SUEX exposure, and slow oven ramping during both post-exposure bake and hard bake. When these mitigations were used together, the SU-8/SUEX stack reproducibly yielded flat, adherent substrates suitable for hybrid mold assembly.

The 3D-printed resin inserts are deliberately used for the features that resin printing can produce reliably: millimeter-scale wells, tapers, skirts, and alignment pins. Micron-scale channels remain photolithographic.

Well inserts are printed using an Elegoo Mars 3 Pro resin printer and a high-temperature resin (Siraya Tech Sculpt, Clear). The manufacturer reports a heat deflection temperature of 180°C for Sculpt Clear/Grey, above the 100°C PDMS cure used here (Siraya Tech, 2026). The insert STL is exported with the same in-plane PDMS shrinkage-compensation factor as the photomask layers, so the insert pins and wafer locks remain in the same compensated coordinate system. No additional manuscript-level correction for printed-resin shrinkage is applied; the design instead uses 150 µm total pin-lock clearance for lateral seating, and printed pin-height variation is checked empirically across the array before bonding (Supplementary Table S1). The demonstrated insert print profile used 20 µm layers, 5.5 s normal exposure, 50 s bottom exposure, 7 bottom layers, anti-aliasing enabled at grey level 2, and no slicer shrinkage or tolerance compensation; complete lift/retract, rest-time, PWM, washing, and UV post-cure settings are provided in Supplementary Table S4. These settings are treated as printer- and resin-specific process parameters rather than design-generator parameters.

Well inserts are printed as an array such that each insert contains a protruding alignment pin (“key”). The SU-8 design contains corresponding holes (“locks”) at the intended insert locations. Insert height sets the molded well depth during PDMS casting.

To eliminate manual placement of individual inserts, inserts are printed in their correct relative positions on a detachable magnetic build plate (or equivalent transfer fixture). The insert array is then transferred onto a larger bonding plate suitable for clamping against a full wafer.

Low-viscosity epoxy is applied in excess to a cavity on the underside (pin side) of each insert. Using excess epoxy makes glue placement less sensitive and allows repeated alignment attempts while seating the pins, because uncured squeeze-out can be washed away before it gels and contaminates the SU-8 microfeatures. The wafer is placed with SU-8 features facing the insert pins and is aligned until all pins seat into their corresponding SU-8-defined holes, providing self-alignment across the full insert array. A second flat plate is placed above the wafer, and uniform pressure is applied using clamps to spread epoxy at the insert-wafer interface.

The lock-and-key alignment is designed with 150 µm total clearance: 1.85 × 1.85 mm pins seat into 2.0 × 2.0 mm square holes (75 µm per side). This tolerance accommodates expected fabrication variation in both SU-8 photolithography and printed-resin fabrication while providing CAD-constrained mechanical registration across the full insert array. In this study, lateral registration is inferred from the seating geometry and successful assembly; Supplementary Table S1 reports z-height variability rather than lateral pin-position metrology.

To compensate for measured print-to-print and across-build-area variation in insert pin height (z) (Supplementary Table S1; peak-to-peak variation 116 µm across one 8×12 array), we place an EPDM rubber sheet (0.03125 in thickness, 60A durometer) between the fixed magnet on the clamping build plate and the removable magnetic build plate carrying the inserts. This compliance equalizes pressure across the array and allows locally taller inserts to sink into the rubber under clamping, helping ensure all pins fully seat and the bonded insert-wafer interface remains flat. The compliant layer converts the measured print-height spread into a fixture-tolerated assembly variable; the 116 µm peak-to-peak variation was accommodated without active leveling or custom machined adjustment.

We use EPO-TEK 301-2 epoxy and follow the manufacturer’s minimum alternative cure schedule at room temperature to keep the clamped insert-wafer interface mechanically stable during cure. Heating the assembly can both change the clamping state and transiently lower epoxy viscosity before gelation, increasing the risk of epoxy squeeze-out or leakage onto SU-8 microfeatures. After clamping and alignment, the assembly is submerged in an acetone bath for 1 min, followed by a second 1 min acetone wash to remove excess epoxy that may have leaked onto the SU-8 microfeatures. The assembly is then dried using a dry air gun and cured at room temperature for 48 h. After cure, the bonded insert-wafer assembly is sonicated in acetone for 10 min to remove any residual uncured epoxy not eliminated during the initial washes.

During process development, incomplete seating of pins due to z-height variation across the printed array was the main bonding failure mode before the compliant clamping layer was added. Nonuniform clamping at the insert-wafer interface, epoxy leakage onto the SU-8 microfeatures, and stress-induced seal loss during cure were addressed by the lock-and-key geometry, acetone cleanup, and room-temperature cure described above. The detachable magnetic build plate also simplified post-cure release of the bonded insert array from the transfer fixture without disturbing insert alignment.

After insert bonding, the assembled hybrid mold is coated with 1 µm parylene C. The coating provides a conformal barrier between the 3D-printed resin/adhesive and downstream PDMS casting and cell culture, reducing exposure to potentially cytotoxic printed-material residues, and it facilitates PDMS release during demolding, which helps preserve mold usability over repeated casts (Musgrove et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2013). Nominal microchannel dimensions reported for the demonstrated designs are pre-coating CAD dimensions; final post-parylene channel dimensions were not measured in this study. Parylene deposition was performed using the Specialty Coating Systems (SCS) 200 parylene coater available at the McGill Nanotools Micro, Nanofabrication Facility.

### PDMS device casting, packaging, and preparation for culture
PDMS (Sylgard 184) is mixed at a 10:1 base:curing-agent ratio, degassed until visible bubbles are removed (typically 10-30 min; approximately −15 inHg in our setup), and cured for 1 h at 100°C. This cure temperature is below both the manufacturer-reported heat deflection temperature of the printed resin and the thermal stability range reported for EPO-TEK 301-2, for which the technical data sheet lists 0.01% weight loss at 200°C and an intermittent operating temperature below 300°C (Epoxy Technology, 2021; Siraya Tech, 2026). PDMS is cast onto the hybrid mold such that the tall insert features define the wells during curing. Casting can be parallelized by pouring, degassing, and curing multiple molds in parallel. No silanization is used; the parylene-coated mold improves PDMS release and provides insulation. After curing and demolding, the device is bonded to a substrate (e.g., glass) following standard plasma bonding protocols.

To improve demolding reliability, macro-scale mold features are designed with draft angles and tapered walls rather than vertical walls, consistent with prior insert-assisted mold designs. During demolding, the wafer is supported on a flat surface while force is applied to reduce snapping. We typically initiate demolding from corners and progress toward the center, pulling not only upward but also backward to stretch the PDMS and reduce local peel stress.

Because PDMS shrinks at elevated curing temperatures, device geometry can be uniformly scaled to compensate; the generator includes built-in support for applying a shrinkage-compensation scale factor during export.

To facilitate rapid, repeatable device packaging, the mold includes a rectangular cutting guide surrounding the array. After demolding, devices are trimmed to a standardized rectangular footprint using an industrial paper guillotine aligned to this guide.

The trimmed PDMS device is bonded to a 110 × 74 mm coverslip-glass sheet. The PDMS-on-glass assembly is autoclaved in sterilization pouches using a dry 121°C cycle and then mounted into a 3D-printed plastic frame. Before assembly, the frame is sterilized with 70% ethanol. Loctite 5140 is dispensed into the frame grooves (typically 0.5-1 mL per frame), and the bonded device is aligned and seated onto the adhesive interface. The adhesive is then cured at room temperature for 3 days before use, after which excess cured adhesive is trimmed away with a razor blade.

3D models for the frame are generated as part of the same output file set. Among the materials evaluated for frame printing, high-impact polystyrene (HIPS; polystyrene modified with rubber) was selected because it combines low hygroscopicity with lower thermal contraction than polypropylene, the only other low-hygroscopicity filament we evaluated. HIPS is also chemically similar to the polystyrene used in standard cell-culture plasticware.

Frames are printed using a Creality K1C printer with HIPS filament (e.g., Filamentum or eSUN). To reduce frame warping during fabrication, printing is performed in an enclosure with part-cooling fans turned off and reduced print speed.

After Loctite curing, assembled devices are plasma-cleaned using a Harrick PDC-001 benchtop plasma system (high power; 30 W, 10 min, 400 mTorr) to further sterilize the device and render surfaces hydrophilic before coating and neuronal culture. Dry ambient air is used as the process gas to control for humidity.

### Directional fluidic-bias testing and plate-format imaging
To assess multi-day directional fluidic bias prior to biological experiments, assembled devices were loaded with 50 µL of Alexa Fluor 488 solution in one compartment and 25 µL of Alexa Fluor 568 solution in the opposing compartment. Devices were imaged immediately after loading and again after 3 days to assess dye crossover between compartments under this imposed volume asymmetry. This was a qualitative visualization test; calibrated compartment fluorescence ratios and a quantitative permeability or isolation coefficient were not measured.

Plate-format imaging compatibility was evaluated on Opera Phenix and ImageXpress high-content screening microscopes using the assembled framed devices. Autofocus and acquisition were tested without custom plate handling or instrument modification.

### Primary cortical neuron culture and distal CTB uptake
Primary cortical neurons were isolated from one E18 Sprague Dawley embryo using the established cortical-neuron preparation described by Harris et al. (2007), then distributed across three separately assembled plates. The three plates are technical plate replicates from one biological preparation, not independent biological replicates. Wells were coated with 30 µL of 10 µg/mL dPGA for 10 min and washed once with water. Neurons were seeded into the soma compartment in 50 µL DMEM + 10% FBS at 5,000-10,000 cells per well, allowed to attach for at least 30 min, and then switched to supplemented Neurobasal Plus medium (1% N2, 2% B27, 1% glutamine). A 50 µL volume was maintained in the soma compartment and 25 µL in the distal compartment to impose a directional soma-to-distal hydrostatic bias during culture.

Cultures were maintained to DIV11. At DIV10, Alexa Fluor-conjugated cholera toxin subunit B (CTB-647) was applied to the lower-volume distal compartment at 1 µg/mL. After 24 h, CTB-positive neuronal cell bodies were counted from endpoint images using a common analysis rule across the three plates. CTB-positive soma count was the sole quantitative biological endpoint. The 6 × 8 device layout contains 24 interior and 24 perimeter devices per plate. Across three plates, all 72 perimeter positions were excluded from this endpoint because the filament-printed frame did not maintain equivalent long-term culture conditions at the plate edge. Of the 72 predefined interior positions, three were excluded because late-stage microbial contamination or an observable technical failure in culture or image acquisition rendered the count uninterpretable; count magnitude alone was not used as an exclusion criterion. The remaining 69 interior device positions were summarized. The submission-facing procedure is provided in Supplementary Protocol S1, while the complete chemical-axotomy workflow used during platform development is retained separately as an archival protocol.

### Literature-derived design-file examples
Three literature-derived layouts were used as design-generation examples: an oligodendrocyte myelination platform based on Wang et al. (2018), an axon guidance chamber with orthogonal gradients adapted from Taylor et al. (2015), and a three-compartment assay adapted from Coquinco et al. (2014). Each layout was encoded with the same OpenMFD file-generation infrastructure used for the neuronal culture device, and OpenMFD was used to generate the corresponding multi-layer photomask DXFs, 3D-printable well-insert CAD/STL outputs, shrinkage-compensated wafer-mask layouts, and wall CAD from the same pipeline used for Figure 1. These examples were not fabricated or biologically validated in this study; their dimensions are therefore treated as a design-generation check against the demonstrated constraints summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

## Results
We evaluated the workflow at the points that determine whether a new microfluidic format is useful for cell biology: whether devices can be produced reproducibly, handled like a plate, imaged on standard high-content microscopes, maintained with directional reagent bias under defined loading conditions, and used for long-term primary neuron culture.

### Hybrid SU-8/3D-printed resin molds cast wells without punching
Manual punching is a throughput bottleneck that introduces variable well placement, edge damage, and handling defects. We first tested whether the hybrid mold workflow could remove this post-processing step by producing a reusable mold that combines SU-8-defined microfeatures with tall, plate-format well structures. The finished mold integrates millimeter-scale insert features with SU-8 microfeatures on a silicon wafer. During PDMS casting, the insert macros form the wells in place, eliminating manual punching and associated variability.

(D) Single and arrayed 3D-printable resin insert STLs. (E) Plate-frame STL for the final package. (F) Assembled framed device.](figures/rendered/openmfd_design.pdf)

Process development defined the controls needed for reliable mold fabrication. Development wafers were used to identify and control the dominant defects: insert delamination, SUEX surface non-flatness that prevented epoxy sealing, and SU-8 microchannel delamination. Omitting any one of the key mitigations below reproducibly produced visible defects before or during insert bonding, whereas applying the full protocol yielded functional molds suitable for repeated PDMS casting.

Key mitigations identified through iterative development include: (i) LP360 long-pass filtration during SUEX exposure to eliminate ridge artifacts that compromise insert-wafer sealing (Supplementary Note S1), (ii) flood-exposed SU-8 base layer to prevent microchannel delamination during development (Supplementary Note S2), (iii) slow thermal ramping (1-3°C/min) during SUEX post-exposure bake and hard bake to prevent stress-induced delamination, (iv) a simple compliant rubber layer in the bonding fixture to accommodate insert height variation and ensure uniform pressure distribution across the array (Supplementary Table S1), (v) detachable magnetic build plate that enables post-cure disassembly by flexing the thin magnetic sheet to release bonded inserts from the transfer fixture, and (vi) room-temperature epoxy cure to minimize thermally induced stress at the bonded interface.

Five wafer-scale hybrid molds were attempted in the reported series. Three consecutive molds completed visible fabrication QC, entered routine use, and produced usable PDMS casts. Two additional wafer assemblies were mechanically lost during downstream casting and handling, one during demolding and one while freeing a wafer from the casting rack after an aluminum-foil leak allowed PDMS to cure underneath the wafer. Across the three routine-use molds, more than 50 casts demolded intact, retained open wells, and showed no visible failures attributable to insert bonding, SU-8/SUEX delamination, or loss of well fidelity. Because every pour was not prospectively logged during process development, the denominator required to calculate a per-cast yield is unavailable. The observed mold and cast outcomes and the definition of a usable cast are reported in Supplementary Table S5. The most recent molds had remained in active use for more than 8 months without observable degradation at the time of manuscript preparation.

### Cast devices are compatible with plate-format handling and imaging
For biological use, the device must fit into existing plate workflows rather than requiring a custom pipetting or imaging setup. After confirming mold fabrication, we evaluated the downstream consequence of casting wells directly into the device: the workflow shifts fabrication from serial manual post-processing to batch production of plate-format arrays. Throughput is therefore limited primarily by how many molds can be poured, degassed, and cured in parallel, rather than by per-device well punching. By integrating wells during PDMS casting rather than punching them after cure, the resulting devices adopt standard microtiter plate spacing and are compatible with multichannel pipettes and plate-format imaging systems in the tested workflows.

![Figure 3: Post-mold device replication and assembly. A completed parylene-coated hybrid mold supports repeated PDMS casting, demolding, trimming, glass bonding, sterilization, frame assembly, adhesive cure, and final plasma cleaning. One-time mold fabrication is detailed in Supplementary Protocol S2.](figures/rendered/mold_casts_package.pdf)

We verified plate-format imaging compatibility in high-content screening workflows by imaging devices on Opera Phenix and ImageXpress HCS microscopes, where plate-reader autofocus operated across the generated well array without custom plate handling or instrument modification.

For compartmentalized assays, plate compatibility is useful only if reagents can remain directionally biased between compartments over the timescale of culture manipulations. We evaluated this qualitatively using a 3-day dye test in which Alexa Fluor 488 (50 µL) and Alexa Fluor 568 (25 µL) were loaded into opposing compartments. Under these asymmetric loading conditions, the endpoint images showed Alexa 488 crossover in the imposed direction and little visible reverse transfer of Alexa 568. Because calibrated compartment fluorescence ratios were not acquired, this observation supports directional hydrostatic bias under the tested loading condition but does not establish a quantitative isolation coefficient or permeability.

### Minimal biological validation demonstrates distal CTB uptake
The biological experiment was designed as a functional check of the fabricated and packaged device, not as a test of a new biological hypothesis. One E18 primary cortical-neuron preparation was distributed across three separately assembled plates and maintained to DIV11. CTB-647 added to the lower-volume distal compartment produced retrogradely labeled neuronal cell bodies in the soma compartment, demonstrating that axons reached the distal compartment and remained accessible to a standard uptake and tracing reagent.

The three plates contained 144 device positions in total. The 72 perimeter positions were excluded a priori because of the frame-dependent culture limitation. Three of the 72 predefined interior positions were then excluded because of visible late-stage contamination or an observable technical failure that made the endpoint image uninterpretable, leaving 69 interior positions for the descriptive CTB-positive-soma summary. Figure 4C shows the mean and standard deviation within each technical plate replicate. No inferential comparison among plates was used because all three plates originated from one donor preparation.

The principal biological limitation arose from the filament-printed plate frame rather than the hybrid mold or cast PDMS device. The frame did not provide a sufficiently tight perimeter seal to maintain equivalent long-term culture conditions in outer positions. Biological readouts were therefore restricted to eligible interior devices. A denser, dimensionally standardized injection-molded carrier is a plausible route to recovering the outer positions, but this packaging solution remains to be tested.

Together, the dye-bias and CTB-uptake results provide a bounded functional validation: the packaged device supports directional fluidic bias, culture to DIV11, distal axon access, and endpoint plate-format imaging in eligible interior positions (Figure 4).

### Literature-derived layouts generate matched fabrication files
Three literature-derived layouts were adapted using the same file-generation infrastructure and design-rule framework as the neuronal culture device.

The three examples were (i) an oligodendrocyte myelination platform based on Wang et al. (2018), (ii) an axon guidance chamber with orthogonal gradients adapted from Taylor et al. (2015), and (iii) a three-compartment assay adapted from Coquinco et al. (2014). For each design, the software generated the same coordinated output classes used for the demonstrated device: multi-layer photomask DXFs, 3D-printable well-insert CAD/STL outputs with lock-and-key registration features, shrinkage-compensated wafer-mask layouts, and wall CAD for final assembly.

These examples were not fabricated. They show that published layouts can generate the mask, insert, and frame files required by the approach when encoded within the OpenMFD rule set; because their layout topology, unit pitch, and channel architecture differ from the validated neuronal device, they should be treated as design-file examples that require local physical validation before biological use.

## Discussion
Wafer-bonded 3D-printed resin inserts provide a practical route from established low-throughput PDMS microfluidic geometries to plate-format devices without replacing SU-8 photolithography or relying on industrial hard-plastic fabrication. The key change is where the wells are made: instead of punching each cured PDMS device, tall 3D-printed resin insert features are bonded to the wafer mold once and then reused to cast wells into every device. This preserves the resolution advantage of SU-8, removes manual well punching, and makes the reusable mold the bridge between one-time facility fabrication and benchtop device replication.

### Fixed fabrication workflow and design automation
The platform separates what changes between designs from what stays fixed during fabrication. OpenMFD converts editable design parameters into coordinated photomask, insert, and frame files, while the physical protocol uses those files without changing the manual sequence. The demonstrated compartmentalized-neuron device validates one complete run of this sequence, including mold fabrication, PDMS casting, packaging, imaging compatibility, directional fluidic bias, and culture. The literature-derived layouts in Figure 5 generate the same file classes but were not fabricated in this study; parameter changes outside the validated neuronal-device implementation are software-supported but require local physical validation.

A central set of process variables needed to be controlled during mold development. Omitting the key controls described above reproducibly led to visible defects such as non-flat SUEX surfaces, microchannel delamination, incomplete insert seating, or bond loss. Once these variables were controlled, the protocol produced repeatable molds and devices. Quality control was also simplified: problematic molds generally failed with readily visible defects before biological use, rather than producing subtle intermediate cases.

A design goal of this platform is to support plate-format adaptations of established microfluidic assays rather than requiring de novo device development. Many proven PDMS microfluidic geometries, including compartmented chambers (Taylor et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2018), gradient generators (Taylor et al., 2015), and co-culture platforms (Coquinco et al., 2014), remain in small-format implementations because fabrication methods that scale to plate formats often trade microfeature resolution for throughput or require expensive tooling. By decoupling fabrication scale from geometric complexity, our approach allows literature-validated architectures to be expressed as plate-format design inputs while preserving the core microfluidic geometry. The software automates this translation: published device dimensions can be provided as parameters, and matched photomask layouts, well-insert arrays, shrinkage compensation, and package outlines are generated from the same coordinate system.

The method has two phases: facility-based mold fabrication and benchtop device replication. SU-8/SUEX patterning, insert bonding, and parylene coating are performed once per mold design; subsequent production uses PDMS casting and assembly. In this study, this amortization was demonstrated directly: three consecutive molds that passed visible QC produced >50 usable casts, and the most recent molds remained active for >8 months without observable degradation. Because the specialized steps are concentrated in the reusable mold rather than repeated for every device, facility-dependent work is performed once during mold fabrication. If parylene deposition is not available locally, the completed hybrid mold can be sent to an external university core or commercial coating service for this final conformal barrier step. After the coated mold is available, routine device production is a benchtop PDMS workflow using standard casting, curing, plasma bonding, and assembly steps.

Although we demonstrate the platform using a neuronal compartmentalization assay, the fabrication strategy is not specific to that application. The same combination of SU-8-defined microfeatures and cast-in-place wells may also suit other compartmented or gradient-based PDMS devices, provided their layouts remain within the demonstrated alignment, demolding, and plate-format window described above.

The primary practical limits are geometric and packaging-related: (i) dimensional tolerances and printed-resin shrinkage that affect alignment and sealing, (ii) channel proximity to the insert-wafer interface, and (iii) perimeter sealing by the current filament-printed plate frame. The adhesive bond and acetone cleanup are handled during one-time cleanroom mold fabrication and did not limit reuse in the demonstrated molds, which supported >50 casts without insert-bonding failures. In the current implementation, the FDM-printed HIPS frame did not maintain equivalent long-term culture conditions in outer positions, so those positions were excluded from the biological endpoint. This frame is a modular packaging component rather than part of the hybrid mold technology. An injection-molded carrier could provide a denser and more dimensionally reproducible perimeter seal while retaining the same PDMS device and reusable mold, but this solution was not evaluated here and remains an open packaging problem.

### Practical design limits
The method depends on keeping several coupled dimensions aligned: the well pitch and plate footprint, photolithography layers, insert pins and wafer locks, insert taper and demolding draft, PDMS shrinkage scale factor, and final package outline. Editing any one of these features manually can create subtle mismatches that become apparent during wafer processing, insert bonding, demolding, or plate assembly.

The generator keeps these coupled features in the same coordinate system and regenerates the matched photomask, insert, wafer-mask, and frame files after parameter changes. The 18 × 9 mm two-well unit pitch, 9 mm well grid, and SUEX-200 design used here should therefore be treated as demonstrated nominal values and practical starting points rather than hard process limits. Reducing pin-hole clearance, shrinking adhesive contact lands, moving channels closer to insert edges, changing SUEX thickness, or altering the plate/package envelope should be treated as new process development and verified on the local photolithography, printed-resin fabrication, and bonding workflow. The key demonstrated dimensions and local validation triggers are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

## Conclusion
Moving well formation into the reusable mold converts SU-8-defined PDMS microfluidic designs into plate-format devices with cast-in-place wells. Shared parameters keep the photomask DXFs, well-insert STLs, and plate-frame CAD coordinated, while wafer-bonded 3D-printed resin inserts eliminate manual well punching during device replication. In the demonstrated implementation, the hybrid molds supported repeated PDMS casting and yielded framed devices compatible with high-content plate microscopes and compartmentalized E18 rat cortical neuron culture with retrograde CTB tracing in analyzed interior positions. The method requires one-time facility access to fabricate each mold design, and parylene coating can be performed locally or by an external coating service, while replication after that point is a benchtop PDMS workflow. For labs that already use SU-8 and PDMS, this provides a practical path from low-throughput custom geometries to plate-format devices without industrial hard-plastic fabrication; method constraints are primarily the demonstrated size and tolerance window described above and the current modular filament-printed frame.
