a b s t r a c t
Synaptic competition is widely believed to be central to the formation and function of neuronal networks, yet the
underlying mechanisms are poorly described. To investigate synaptic competition in vitro, we have developed a
novel two input pathway competition model using a 3-compartment microﬂuidic device. Axons from cultured
rat cortical neurons from two different lateral compartments (inputs) innervate a common neuronal population
in a separate central compartment. Inhibiting one input's activity, using the GABAAR agonist muscimol, resulted
in increased synapse numbers and axon elongation of the opposing untreated (uninhibited) inputs in the central
compartment. Time lapse imaging revealed that uninhibited inputs outgrew and outconnected their inhibited
counterparts. This form of competition occurs during a sensitive period ending prior to 21 DIV and is NMDAR
and CamKII dependent. Surprisingly, this form of plasticity was dependent on the age of the center compartment
neurons but not of the competing inputs.

Introduction
In the developing brain, neurons undergo a substantial number of
morphological and synaptic changes (Hua and Smith, 2004; Katz and
Shatz, 1996). A constant balance of synapse formation, stabilization
and elimination is required for the proper development and formation
of neural circuits (Hua and Smith, 2004). A major contribution to
these mechanisms is driven by the experience/activity/input that the
brain receives during development (Hensch, 2005). One extensively
studied example of this phenomenon occurs in the development of
the visual cortex. When one eye is closed during development, electrophysiological observations show increases in activity and territory in
favor of the open eye (Hensch, 2004; Hubel and Wiesel, 1970). These
changes are also readily observable with anatomical techniques which
show wider ocular dominance columns associated with the nondeprived eye than those related to the deprived eye (Gordon and
Stryker, 1996; Wiesel and Hubel, 1963). The mechanism by which
neural activity shapes neural circuitry during development remains a
key question to be resolved in neuroscience research.
While visual cortex plasticity is one of the more well studied models
of neural development, it is not without its own difﬁculties. Because of

the complexity of the mammalian brain, it is difﬁcult to observe changes
on cellular and molecular scales. The use of primary neuronal cultures
allows for easier observation and genetic manipulation of neurons
(Gartner et al., 2006; Okabe et al., 2001; Zeitelhofer et al., 2009). However, changing the activity of neurons in culture is limited to nonselective chemical treatment of the entire culture or genetic modiﬁcation
of a small population of neurons within the culture (Adesnik et al.,
2008; Burrone et al., 2002; Harms et al., 2005; Lee et al., 1998; Lu
et al., 2001; Otmakhov et al., 2004).
Microﬂuidic technology now provides us with the ability to grow
neurons in distinct chemical environments. Microﬂuidics is a ﬁeld of
study utilizing minute volumes to control and manipulate ﬂuid movement to create chemically isolated environments (Park et al., 2006;
Whitesides, 2006). By manipulating the volumes of the individual compartments, distinct chemical environments can be created due to the
hydrostatic forces present between compartments. Park et al. (2006)
fabricated a two compartment microﬂuidic device in which two neuronal groups could remain chemically isolated but were still allowed to
interact with each other through their axonal projections. Here, we
use a three compartment microﬂuidic device to create a new dual
input model to study in vitro synaptic competition.

Muscimol induced inhibition causes a shift in synapse distribution
To ensure that chronic inhibition with muscimol did not lead to
desensitization of the GABA receptors, acute (Fig. 2a) and chronic
(Fig. 2b) inhibition of neuronal activity with this treatment was
conﬁrmed by electrophysiological whole-cell patch clamp recording of
cortical cells in standard culture conditions. In all cases, spontaneous
network burst ﬁring was rapidly eliminated by muscimol treatment,
whereas quantal glutamate transmission and synaptic connectivity
were retained. These changes remained constant even when assessed
48 h later. The data demonstrate that muscimol treatment silences all
synchronous activity while synaptic connectivity remains intact.
At DIV 12–14, muscimol (10 μM) was applied to one lateral compartment for 48 h. Following muscimol treatment neurons were ﬁxed (1%
PFA followed by methanol) and the center compartment (target) neurons were immunostained for the postsynaptic marker, PSD-95 (Afﬁnity
BioReagents). Axons from the muscimol treated compartment formed

fewer synapses (as deﬁned by colocalized synaptophysin and PSD-95
clusters) than axons from the untreated compartment (Fig. 3, 15.41 ±
2.40 and 28.69 ± 4.19 synapses/ﬁeld respectively; t-test, p b 0.0078,
n = 32). Control cultures, in which neither lateral compartment was
treated with muscimol, had synapse numbers similar to those from
the muscimol treated compartment (Fig. 3a, ANOVA: p b 0.0001,
Bonferroni post-test, where ncontrol = 90). This surprising result indicates that treatment of one input with muscimol results in enhanced
synapse numbers from the opposing compartment's inputs.
To identify potential mechanisms underlying this difference, we
interfered with several signaling pathways only within the center compartment (Fig. 4a). NMDARs are central players in activity dependent
developmental competition due to their role as “Hebbian” coincidence
detectors (Cull-Candy, 2004) and their subunit composition changes
as neurons mature (Liu et al., 2004). Application of the NMDAR antagonist, APV (50 μM), speciﬁcally to neurons in the center compartment
(Fig. 4c, left), prevented the muscimol induced synaptic shift (muscimol
treated: 12.13 ± 2.20 synapses/ﬁeld, untreated: 12.29 ± 1.57 synapses/
ﬁeld; t-test: p = 0.95, n = 38). NMDAR activity leads to the activation
of CamKII (Bayer et al., 2001) which in turn results in AMPAR phosphorylation and enhanced insertion into the postsynaptic membrane
(Malinow and Malenka, 2002); a candidate mechanism for increases
in synaptic weighting such as LTP. Treatment of the center compartment
neurons with a CamKII inhibitor, KN-93 (1 μM) (Fig. 4c, middle), also
prevented the competitive effects induced by muscimol application
(muscimol treated: 11.14 ± 1.79 synapses/ﬁeld, untreated: 12.32 ±
1.33 synapses/ﬁeld; t-test: p = 0.60, n = 37). Previous studies have
reported a link between long term depression and synapse elimination
(Bastrikova et al., 2008) and prevention of GluA2 receptor internalization

Surprisingly, no signiﬁcant difference in synapse density (deﬁned as
the number of synapses per 10 μm of axon length) between the treated
(0.210 ± 0.041 synapses/10 μm, n = 25) and untreated (0.309 ±
0.058 synapses/10 μm, n = 25) inputs was observed (ANOVA = 0.33;
control: 0.265 ± 0.36 synapses/10 μm, n = 33; Fig. 5a). This suggests
that the observed difference in synapse number between treated and
untreated inputs might be due to differences in axonal length and,
therefore, growth. To examine this point directly, we assessed axonal
extension and motility using time lapse imaging at 30 min intervals.
The overall motility of axons (observed over 10–20 h during the
muscimol treatment period) originating from the treated and untreated
lateral compartments in the center compartment was classiﬁed into
three categories: advancing, retracting and unchanged. At this age
(DIV 12–14), independent of treatment, both sets of lateral compartment axons showed a net positive growth. However, the degree of
axonal growth was different for the two incoming groups of axons.
Afferents originating from the untreated compartment (9.25 ±
0.88 μm/30 min, n = 11) on average moved twice as fast and farther
than those from the muscimol treated compartment (4.99 ± 0.58 μm/
30 min, n = 11). The average growth rate of axons from the muscimol

treated compartment was not signiﬁcantly different from that of axons
in cultures in which neither lateral compartment was treated with
muscimol (6.34 ± 0.73 μm/30 min, n = 17). Rather axons from the
untreated compartment increased their outgrowth and synapse number
when opposed by muscimol treated axons (Fig. 5b, ANOVA p = 0.0025,
Bonferroni post-test). The surprising result is that untreated inputs
undergo enhanced axon growth in a competitive environment when
the activity of a competing group of inputs is reduced by muscimol
application.
Changes in synapse distribution induced by muscimol no longer occur by
3 weeks in vitro
To determine whether developmental age plays a role in this process, 48 h muscimol treatments were initiated at DIV 8, 19 and 26
(Fig. 6b–c, d–e, f–g, respectively). Muscimol treatment from DIV 8–10,
led to a signiﬁcant difference in the synapse number between treated
and untreated lateral compartment axons (muscimol treated: 3.14 ±
0.71 synapses/ﬁeld; untreated: 6.60 ± 0.67 synapses/ﬁeld; t-test:
p = 0.0007; n = 35). However, there were no effects at the later ages
of DIV 19–21 (muscimol treated: 16.56 ± 2.62 synapses/ﬁeld; untreated:
20.86 ± 2.79 synapses/ﬁeld; t-test p = 0.26, n = 36) or DIV 26–28
(muscimol treated: 20.38 ± 2.75 synapses/ﬁeld; untreated: 17.71 ±
1.78 synapses/ﬁeld; t-test: p = 0.42, n = 34). These experiments demonstrate that the age of the cultures is an important variable in this
model (Fig. 6a) and suggests the existence of a sensitive period.

Discussion
We used a 3-compartment microﬂuidic device to create a novel
in vitro dual input synaptic competition model. Reducing the activity
of inputs from one lateral compartment, while leaving the other
unchanged, results in increased synapse numbers associated with the
untreated input afferents. These effects are due primarily to untreated
axons advancing further/more rapidly in the center (target) compartment than muscimol treated axons as opposed to increases in synapse
density along the axon. One possible explanation is that the stabilization
of presynaptic markers such as synaptophysin correlates with further
axonal branching and growth (Ruthazer et al., 2006) and these sites
are more likely candidates for becoming stable presynaptic terminals
for synapses (Sabo et al., 2006). Therefore, the increased axonal growth

in our model may be due to the increased stability of synaptic
connections on ‘winning’ afferents which then promotes further axonal
branching and growth.
The mechanisms underlying these changes involve NMDAR
activation and CamKII dependent signaling; however, further work is
required to determine whether LTP-like AMPAR membrane insertions
play a signiﬁcant role in this competition model. Furthermore, the
changes appear independent of GluR2 internalization and LTD-like
processes. LTD is associated with synapse elimination and retraction
(Bastrikova et al., 2008) while our model appears to predominantly
involve increased growth. L-type calcium channels have also been
shown to have a role in activity dependent synaptic plasticity (Goold
and Nicoll, 2010; Wang et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2008). Future studies
examining their role will help to further our understanding of synaptic
competition.
Previous experiments have shown that synapse formation and
maturation in cultured neurons occur most rapidly at approximately
two weeks in culture (Craig et al., 1993). The effects observed here
were found to be age dependent, suggesting a sensitive period akin to

also form synapses with lateral compartment dendrites, and the roles

Our study has demonstrated that decreased neuronal activity within
one population of incoming afferents can inﬂuence the synapse formation and growth of axons of a competing neuronal population. The
experimental model we introduce here has many potential uses in the
study of activity dependent synaptic plasticity.
Experimental methods
Microﬂuidic devices
Microﬂuidic devices were constructed as detailed in (Park et al.,
2006) with an additional center compartment 0.5 mm in width. Devices
were soaked in PBS, rinsed in water and dried prior to assembly. Devices
were then placed onto No. 1.5 glass coverslips. Assembled devices were
ﬁlled with poly-D-lysine and kept at 37 °C for at least 24 h. Devices were

then washed with distilled water and kept at 37 °C (while compartments were still ﬁlled with water) until use.

Cell culture and treatment
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care
Committee of the University of British Columbia. Primary cortical
neuronal cultures were prepared from Wistar rats' (University of
British Columbia Animal Care Centre, Vancouver, BC, Canada) brains at
embryonic days 18–19. Cultures were maintained in Neurobasal
medium supplemented with B27, 0.5 mm Glutamax (Invitrogen) and
Penicillin–Streptomycin (Invitrogen).
Transfections were performed via nucleofection at DIV 0 using
plasmids containing the pLL3.7 backbone with synapsin promoter
and expressing synaptophysin-YFP or -mcherry. Freshly dissociated
neurons (4 million) were pelleted for nucleofection using an Amaxa
Nucleofector II (Lonza) program O-003 with 2 μg of plasmid DNA and
then immediately resuspended in 1 mL DMEM (Invitrogen) with 10%
FBS (Invitrogen) for plating.
Plating of neurons into the microﬂuidic devices was done by placing
10 μL of the neuronal cell suspension (4 million cells/mL for transfected
neurons, 3 million cells/mL for nontransfected) into each compartment
of the device. Cells were allowed to ﬂow through the compartment and

Imaging microscopy and analysis
Image acquisition
Images were acquired on a Zeiss Axioplan2 microscope with a 63×
1.4 numerical aperture oil objective and Photometrics Sensys cooled
CCD camera using MetaVue imaging software (Molecular Devices)
and customized ﬁlter sets (immunocytochemistry).
Images were acquired on a Zeiss AxioObserver microscope with a
63 × 1.4 numerical aperture oil objective (immunocytochemistry) or
40 × 0.95 numerical aperture air objective (live imaging) and Zeiss
HRM camera using Axiovision imaging software (Zeiss) and customized
ﬁlter sets.
For immunocytochemistry experiments n equals the number of ﬁeld
images taken from at least 3 independent experiments. All ﬁelds were
taken at the center line of the ‘target’ compartment to prevent any proximity bias between lateral afferent compartments. A ﬁeld is deﬁned as
the ﬁeld of view observed under the microscope (i.e. 1 image taken
under 63× magniﬁcation for the immunocytochemistry experiments).
For time lapse image experiments, n equals the numbers of axons
observed from at least 2 independent experiments. All time lapse
imaging experiments used observations at the center line of the center
compartment to prevent any proximity bias between lateral afferents.
Colocalization image analysis was performed using the ImageJ
colocalization plugin found on the ImageJ website (http://rsbweb.nih.
gov/ij/index.html). Before running the plugin, images were (blindly)
thresholded using ImageJ and binary masks were made to include
only puncta 5–300 pixels in size (1 pixel = 0.1 μm). Axon segments
were (blindly) selected under the criterion that segments were clearly
distinguishable as a single axon from a single source.
Growth classiﬁcation analysis was done by identifying all axon
growth cones in the ﬁeld of view. Growth cones were observed over
the duration of the imaging or until outside the ﬁeld of view.
Axon growth rate was obtained by identifying and selecting axon
growth cones within the ﬁeld of view 4–24 h into treatment. Axons
were observed until imaging concluded or axons moved out of the
ﬁeld of view. Axon growth segments were (blindly) selected under
the criterion that segments were clearly distinguishable as a single
axon from a single source.
All imaging values are described by mean ± SEM.
Electrophysiology
Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on CTX cells at
DIV 12–14, voltage clamp recordings were obtained at Vh − 60 mV
and the membrane test function was used to determine intrinsic
membrane properties ~1 min after obtaining whole-cell conﬁguration,
as described previously (Kaufman et al., 2012; Milnerwood et al.,
2012; Tapia et al., 2012). Brieﬂy, neurones were perfused at room
temperature with extracellular solution (ECS) containing (in mM unless
stated): 167 NaCl, 2.4 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 glucose, 10 HEPES, 2 CaCl2,
